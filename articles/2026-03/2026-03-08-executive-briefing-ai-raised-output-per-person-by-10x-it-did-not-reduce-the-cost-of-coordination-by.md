## You spent 12 hours in meetings last week.

***The problem isn’t the meetings.***

You already know what your week looks like. You open your calendar on Monday morning and the color-coded blocks have eaten everything. Eleven hours in meetings if you’re lucky. Sixteen if you manage people, according to Flowtrace. Twenty-three if you’re an executive, per Harvard Business Review — which means you have seventeen hours left for the work the meetings are supposedly about.

The standup that could be a Slack message. The cross-functional sync where eight people attend and two talk. The alignment session that produces a follow-up alignment session. The sprint review that takes longer to prepare for than the sprint took to run. You’ve been complaining about this for years. Everybody has. The meetings tripled since 2020 — Microsoft’s data, not mine — and nobody can explain where the new ones came from or what most of them accomplish. Seventy-one percent of senior executives say their own meetings are unproductive. U.S. businesses lose an estimated $259 billion a year to meetings that shouldn’t exist.

But the meetings aren’t the disease. They’re the symptom. The disease is that your teams are the wrong size. And by “wrong,” I don’t mean a little too big. I mean 3 to 10 times too big. And every AI tool you adopt — every Copilot, every agent, every automation — is making the problem worse, not better, because you’re amplifying output through a coordination structure that was already broken.

This is the single most important organizational issue in corporate right now, and almost nobody is treating it that way. Team size determines how you spend every hour of your working day — how many meetings you sit in, how many Slack channels you monitor, how many approvals you wait for, how many people you need to align before you can ship anything. It governs whether your best people spend their energy on the work or on the overhead of coordinating the work. It shapes your hiring, your costs, your speed, your culture, and — this is the part that should alarm you — the quality of every decision your organization makes.

And the math on team size just broke. AI broke it. Not in the way most people think.


## The number is five

Before we get to AI, we need to establish something that predates it by thousands of years. Because the reason five works isn’t technological. It’s biological. And that distinction matters enormously for what comes next.

The number of communication pathways between people in a group is n(n-1)/2. Two people: one pathway. Three people: three. Five people: ten. Eight people: twenty-eight. Ten people: forty-five. Twenty people: one hundred and ninety.

At five people, every person can hold the full communication map in their head during a single conversation. They know what everyone else knows. They can anticipate how a decision will land with every team member without calling a meeting to find out. At ten people, this becomes impossible. At twenty, it’s a joke. The pathways aren’t just numerous — they’re active. Each one generates messages, expectations, misunderstandings, context that needs to be maintained. This is why your Slack has seventy channels and your calendar is full. It’s not bad habits. It’s combinatorics.

Robin Dunbar’s research on primate neocortex size established this in 1992, and it’s been validated relentlessly since: the human brain has layered limits on relationship complexity. Five for your core support group. Fifteen for deep trust. Fifty for meaningful working relationships. One hundred and fifty for stable social connections. These aren’t approximations. Army mathematicians confirmed them empirically — the Army Times reported that researchers found groups of five communicated most effectively, with effectiveness peaking again at 15, 50, and 150. Chimpanzees, it turns out, organize in the same clusters.

The military has been testing this with stakes that make quarterly earnings look trivial. A U.S. infantry fire team is four people plus a leader. Two fire teams make a squad. Three squads make a platoon. Three platoons make a company. The numbers at every layer track Dunbar’s hierarchy almost exactly. The structure reflects thousands of years of evolutionary pressure applied to the question: how many people can a leader actually direct in conditions where getting it wrong means people die?

Five.

Jeff Bezos landed on the same number from a completely different direction — the “two-pizza team” of five to eight people. Fred Brooks got there in 1975 through software engineering: his “chief surgeon” model was a small team of elite specialists, because adding people to a project made it slower, not faster. The communication overhead always overwhelmed the added capacity.

Three disciplines. Evolutionary psychology, military operations, software engineering. Same answer. The human brain can sustain deep, trust-based, high-context coordination with about five people. Not ten, not eight — five. The military will stretch to eight when the mission demands it, but the fire team — the actual unit that fights — is four or five.

This was already true before AI. What AI changes is not the number. What AI changes is the consequences of getting the number wrong.


## What AI actually changed

The standard narrative goes like this: AI makes people more productive, so teams can be smaller. That’s true as far as it goes, which isn’t far enough. It’s like saying “the invention of the car made horses less necessary.” Technically correct. Utterly insufficient for understanding what actually happened to cities, employment, geography, and the entire structure of the economy.

What AI actually changed is more structural than most people realize. Before AI, a five-person team with ten communication pathways might produce X output. The quality of that output was limited by the team’s collective skill and the hours available. Adding a sixth person gave you fifteen communication pathways and, in theory, a bit more output — but with diminishing returns because the coordination overhead grew faster than the capacity. Tobi Lütke of Shopify calls this “a 10x loss of productivity” with each addition beyond five, and whether the exact multiplier is 10x or 5x, the direction is not in dispute.

After AI, the same five-person team produces 5 to 10X. The evidence for this is no longer theoretical. It’s sitting in the revenue-per-employee data of every AI-native company that’s emerged in the last three years:

Lovable launched in late 2024 with a small team. By July 2025, it had hit $100M ARR with just 45 employees — $2.2 million in revenue per person. Their founder told Lenny Rachitsky: “We believe in hiring super high agency people and very technically focused talent. Most of the team are former founders.” By late 2025 they had doubled to $200M ARR.

Midjourney: roughly $500M in 2025 revenue with around 100-160 employees, zero external funding, zero marketing spend. Revenue per employee ranges from $3 to $5 million depending on the headcount estimate — comparable to or exceeding Nvidia’s $3.6M RPE in FY2025. Reached profitability one month after launch.

ElevenLabs: crossed $100M ARR by April 2025 and reached $330M by year’s end. Even with headcount growing to roughly 400, revenue per employee sits around $825K — far above traditional SaaS benchmarks, though below the other two companies on this list.

The traditional SaaS benchmark for “great” revenue per employee, per Kyle Poyar’s widely cited analysis, was $200K-$300K. AI-native companies are running 5 to 10 times that number. Salesforce does roughly $610K per employee. Adobe is similar. These AI-native teams aren’t incrementally better. They’re structurally different organisms.

Now. Here is the thing that reframes everything.

If each person on a five-person team is producing $2-3M per year in revenue, the coordination cost of the sixth person is no longer a minor tax. It’s a catastrophe. You’re not just adding communication overhead. You’re diluting a team that is already producing at an extraordinary rate. That sixth person doesn’t just need to be “good enough.” They need to justify their coordination cost against a baseline where every existing team member is generating output that previously required an entire department.

This is the new math, stated plainly: the penalty for adding a human to a team increases as the per-human output increases. When each person produced $250K per year, the coordination cost of person number six was manageable. When each person produces $2M per year, the coordination cost of person number six is measured in millions of dollars of lost productivity. The bar for that sixth person didn’t rise a little. It rose by an order of magnitude.

And this is why your meetings are killing you. Every meeting exists because someone decided that coordination was worth the cost. In a world where per-person output was $250K, it often was. In a world where per-person output is $2M and the coordination cost hasn’t gotten any cheaper, most of those meetings are net negative. They’re not just wasting time. They’re destroying value at a rate that scales with how productive your people are.


## The variable everyone gets wrong

Every conversation I hear about AI and teams is about volume. More code, more content, more analysis, faster. The implicit assumption is that the binding constraint being relaxed is output quantity — teams couldn’t produce enough, and now with AI they can produce more.

This framing leads to disastrously wrong organizational decisions. Because volume is no longer the scarce resource. AI made volume cheap. What’s scarce — and what determines whether your organization succeeds or fails — is correctness. Whether the thing you shipped is actually right. Architecturally sound. Strategically coherent. Free of the subtle errors that look fine in a demo and compound into catastrophic failures in production.

A Harvard Business School field experiment published as an NBER working paper in 2025 tested this directly. Researchers studied 776 professionals at Procter & Gamble working on real product innovation challenges. The setup: individuals and two-person teams, some with AI and some without, all evaluated by independent experts who didn’t know which group produced which work.

The finding that matters: teams using AI were three times more likely to produce ideas in the top 10% of quality compared to the baseline of individuals working without AI. Not three times more output. Three times more likely to be right at the highest level. Individuals with AI matched the performance of teams without AI — a single person plus AI replicated the quality benefits of human collaboration. But the best results came from teams plus AI. The researchers called AI a “cybernetic teammate.”

There was a second finding that matters even more for the team size question. Without AI, R&D people produced technical solutions and marketing people produced commercial solutions — functional silos. With AI, both groups produced more balanced, integrated ideas. AI broke down the silos, not by adding more people but by extending each person’s competence into adjacent domains with higher accuracy.

This is the mechanism that makes small AI-augmented teams more powerful than large teams without AI. In a team of five excellent people using AI, each person can operate competently across a broader domain than they could alone. The team doesn’t need ten specialists covering ten narrow lanes. It needs five generalist-architects who each use AI to extend into adjacent territory — and who use each other as verification against the AI’s errors.

But verification is the catch. And it’s the catch that makes team size matter more in the AI era, not less.

Every piece of AI output requires human judgment to validate. In a five-person team, each person reviews a manageable volume of AI output against a coherent shared context. They know what “right” looks like because they all hold the same mental model of the product, the architecture, the customer. In a twenty-person team, the AI output multiplies by four but the shared context degrades catastrophically — twenty people cannot maintain a single coherent model of what “right” looks like. So they hold meetings to try to synchronize. And those meetings generate more decisions, which generate more AI tasks, which generate more output to verify, which requires more meetings to align on what “right” means.

Wes McKinney described this as the “agentic tar pit” in his June 2025 essay “The Mythical Agent-Month.” Agent sessions produce contradictory plans that humans must reconcile. AI generates what McKinney calls “technical debt at machine speed.” The working prototype — the demo that appears to function — has become trivially easy to produce. Getting from prototype to production still requires deep human judgment at every step. And the humans doing that judgment need to share a mental model of what they’re building. The larger the team, the weaker that shared model, and the more likely that “fast AI output” actually means “fast production of things that are subtly wrong.”

A team of five optimizes for correctness. A team of twenty optimizes for volume. In a world where AI makes volume virtually free, optimizing for volume is optimizing for the wrong thing.

This is why your big teams feel productive — they produce a lot — and also why they keep shipping things that don’t quite work, need rework, require post-mortems, and spawn follow-up projects to fix the problems created by the last project. Volume masquerades as progress. Correctness is progress.


## When one is the right number

Before I build the framework, I need to address the other end of the spectrum, because there is real evidence that for certain types of work, the right team size isn’t five. It’s one.

Peter Steinberger spent 13 years building PSPDFKit, a PDF toolkit used by Apple, Dropbox, and SAP. Insight Partners backed it at $116 million. He burned out, stepped away, traveled. Then in late 2025, he started tinkering with AI agents as a solo project. Over the course of a few months, running multiple coding agents simultaneously, he built OpenClaw — an open-source AI agent that went viral, racked up massive GitHub traction, and attracted direct outreach from Mark Zuckerberg and Sam Altman. Meta and OpenAI both courted him. He joined OpenAI in February 2026. He chose not to build a company. “What I want is to change the world, not build a large company.”

The details matter. Steinberger built OpenClaw in TypeScript — a language he’d never used. The agents handled syntax while he applied system-level architecture knowledge developed over two decades. He explicitly told Lex Fridman he hadn’t read some of the code he released. He directed agents at the architectural level. They executed. He verified against taste and system coherence.

One person. Twenty years of judgment. A swarm of agents. Output that two of the world’s most valuable companies wanted to acquire.

Dario Amodei, Anthropic’s CEO, put 70-80% odds on a one-person billion-dollar company by 2026 — likely in proprietary trading, dev tools, or automated services. Mike Krieger, Instagram co-founder and formerly Anthropic’s chief product officer, said it wasn’t crazy: “I built a billion-dollar company with 13 people.”

But the one-person case has a specific profile and specific limits. Steinberger was not a novice experimenting with ChatGPT. He brought judgment forged across a $100M+ exit and two decades of system-level engineering. The solo operator model works when the work is exploration — high ambiguity, low coordination requirements, a premium on speed and individual taste. It works in domains where the entire value chain is digital, the product is software, and the regulatory burden is low.

It breaks down when correctness requires multiple perspectives, when the cost of being subtly wrong is high, when sustained production — not a burst of exploration — is the goal. And it doesn’t scale when customers, regulations, partnerships, and operational complexity require more than one brain’s worth of context.

The one-person model is a scout. The five-person model is a strike team. Both are correct for different missions. The problem is that most organizations have neither. They have twelve-person platoons trying to do the work of scouts and strike teams simultaneously, failing at both, and drowning in meetings to manage the confusion.


## Scouts and strike teams

I’ve started using these two archetypes with clients and they land immediately, because they map to how people already intuitively feel about their work — even if they’ve never had language for it.

Scouts operate alone. One person, full AI toolkit, a defined mission, and a deadline. The work is exploration: is this technology viable? Is this market real? Can we build a working prototype of this idea? What would this architecture look like? Scouts move fast because they have zero coordination overhead. They report back with a map of the territory — not a finished product, but a clear picture of what’s possible and what isn’t. Their constraint is that one person’s judgment is the ceiling. No peer review, no redundancy, no error correction from a team that shares context. This is fine for exploration. It’s dangerous for production.

Scout assignments: building a prototype in a week to test a thesis. Evaluating a new model or tool against your actual workflows. Conducting deep research on a strategic question. Stress-testing a competitor’s claims. Writing the first draft of a product specification that a strike team will later execute against.

Strike teams are five people with AI executing against a defined objective where correctness matters. The five-person composition has specific structural advantages that only become visible when you think in terms of correctness rather than volume:

Every person’s AI-generated output passes through at least one other brain that shares enough context to catch real errors — not cosmetic ones, but architectural and strategic ones. Five people can cover product, engineering, design, data, and domain expertise — the minimum viable surface area for a complete product decision. Below five, you have blind spots. Above five, you start developing silos. Five people can maintain a single coherent mental model of what they’re building and what “right” looks like. That shared model is the verification standard against which every piece of AI output is judged. Lose it, and you lose the ability to distinguish good work from merely plausible work. And in a team of five, there is nowhere to hide. Every person’s judgment is tested every day. That’s uncomfortable. It’s the point.

Strike team assignments: building and shipping a production system. Developing a product from validated prototype to market. Executing a strategic initiative with measurable outcomes. Managing a complex client engagement. Anything where the cost of being wrong exceeds the cost of being slow.

The framework is this simple: scouts explore, strike teams execute. Scouts map the territory, strike teams build the road. An organization needs both. Most organizations currently have neither — they have oversized teams that are too slow for exploration and too diluted for precision execution, burning their best people’s hours on the coordination overhead of being the wrong size.


## Scaling strike teams into organizations

This is where I have to be honest about the limits of what anyone currently knows. Because the hardest question isn’t “what’s the right team size?” It’s “how do you compose hundreds of five-person teams into a coherent organization?”

Shopify runs 3,500 R&D people as “lots and lots and lots of small teams.” But Lütke reviews every project monthly against written product principles, and AI analyzes each project for coherence with those principles. That’s a specific, hands-on coordination mechanism that works because Lütke is technically literate enough to evaluate the work and disciplined enough to do it at scale. Most CEOs can’t replicate this. Most orgs don’t have written principles specific enough to be useful. Most companies have mission statements, not constitutions.

What I think we do know, derived from the same biological and mathematical constraints that give us the number five.

The layers scale in Dunbar ratios. Five people make a strike team. Three to four strike teams (15-20 people) share a domain, coordinated by a single person whose job is inter-team coherence — not management, not approval authority, but the maintenance of a shared context across teams working on related problems. Three to four of these clusters (50-80 people) share a strategic objective, led by a small leadership team of three to five. Three to four of these divisions (150-300 people) constitute an organization at the outer boundary of stable human coordination.

These aren’t arbitrary nesting ratios. They’re Dunbar’s layers, the same ones the military has used for centuries: fire team, squad, platoon, company. At each level, the leader can maintain the quality of relationship required for the coordination being asked of them. A strike team leader knows everything about their four teammates. A cluster coordinator can maintain deep trust with 15-20 people across four teams. A division leader can sustain meaningful working relationships with 50-80 people. Beyond 150, you need formal systems — culture, written principles, AI-assisted analysis — because individual human cognition runs out.

I want to be careful not to present this as settled doctrine — nobody has run this playbook at Fortune 500 scale and published the results. But the constraints are real, and the math at least narrows the range of structures that can work.

The management layer thins but the taste layer thickens. Self-directed teams of five experts don’t need engineering managers. They don’t need project managers when AI tracks progress, and most coordination roles dissolve when there are fewer humans to coordinate.

But you desperately need people who maintain taste — what Lütke calls the “constitution.” The non-platitude decisions the company has made, where a reasonable competitor might choose the opposite. The definition of what “correct” looks like for this specific organization, applied against this specific product, for these specific customers. In an organization of federated strike teams producing at 10x with AI, the people who define and enforce the standard of correctness are the most important people in the building. They aren’t managers. They’re curators. And their role becomes more critical, not less, as AI output scales, because AI-generated work that is consistent with the constitution is valuable and AI-generated work that deviates from it is waste — or worse, it’s wrong in a way that looks right.

The coordination function is itself a small team. The group responsible for strategic coherence across strike teams should also be small. Three to five people who define principles, review work against those principles, surface conflicts between teams, and make the judgment calls that require organizational-level context. Think of it less as a PMO and more as a taste team. Their tools are the constitution, AI-powered analysis of team output, and direct access to every team’s work.


## What this means for company size

Follow the math to its conclusion and the result is uncomfortable.

If a strike team of five people with AI can produce what a team of 30 to 50 produced three years ago — and the revenue-per-employee data from Lovable, Midjourney, ElevenLabs, and others strongly suggests this — then the obvious conclusion is that most organizations are five to ten times too big.

That’s the conclusion almost everyone draws. And it is the single least interesting thing you could do with this information.


## The ambition failure

The thing that drives me crazy about the conversation around AI and team size is that everyone — the analysts, the consultants, the board members, the CEOs — frames it as a cost story. “We can do the same work with fewer people.” That’s the headline. That’s the strategy deck. That’s what gets presented at board meetings and discussed at Davos. Same mission. Fewer bodies. Lower burn.

And it’s a staggering failure of imagination.

You have 500 people. Each of them just got five to ten times more capable. The correct response to this is not “I can now run my company with 50 people.” The correct response is “I now have the capacity of 2,500 to 5,000 people. What was I previously unable to do?”

Think about what that means for a moment. Your 500-person company just acquired the productive capacity of a 3,000-person company — without hiring anyone, without raising capital, without building new offices or adding management layers. You did not get a cost reduction. You got an army. Whether you have the strategic vision to deploy that capacity — or whether you’re going to use a fleet of aircraft carriers to run the same fishing route your trawler used to run — is the dividing line.

The companies I advise that are getting this right are not cutting heads. They are recomposing their organizations into strike teams — yes, smaller per team — and then massively expanding the number of fronts they’re fighting on. Markets they never would have entered because the team cost was too high. Products they never would have built because they couldn’t staff the effort. Customer segments they ignored because the economics didn’t work at $300K per employee but work spectacularly at $2M per employee.

A regional insurance company with 200 people currently serves three states. Reorganized as 40 strike teams with AI augmentation, they have the capacity to serve thirty states and build products they outsource today. A mid-market SaaS company with 400 engineers maintains one product. Restructured as 80 strike teams, they can build and maintain a platform with ten products and enter adjacent verticals their competitors thought were protected by headcount moats. A consulting firm with 1,000 people runs 50 engagements. Recomposed as 200 strike teams, they can run 200 engagements simultaneously, enter new practice areas, and serve client segments that were previously uneconomical.

None of these companies need fewer people. They need the same people, reorganized into smaller teams, pointed at a mission five times larger than the one they’re currently pursuing.

This is the part nobody is talking about. Not the headcount reduction. The ambition expansion. The globe-spanning, category-redefining, “we used to compete in one market and now we compete in twelve” ambition that becomes possible when every five-person team has the productive capacity of a fifty-person department.

Where is that conversation? I hear it approximately never. I hear “efficiency.” I hear “do more with less.” I hear “right-sizing.” I never hear a Fortune 500 CEO stand up and say: “We just got handed a 10x multiplier on our workforce. We’re going to 10x our mission.”

The reason nobody says it is the same organizational inertia I’ve written about before. Leaders are building their strategy around the current mission because that’s all they’ve ever known. Their planning processes, their budgeting cycles, their strategic frameworks — all of these assume that headcount is the binding constraint on ambition. They’ve spent their entire careers in a world where “we can’t do that, we don’t have the people” was a valid and final answer to any sufficiently ambitious proposal. That answer just expired. And most of them haven’t noticed.

Lovable didn’t start with 45 people and decide to build a small product. They started with 45 people and decided to build a platform that serves 8 million users across every industry on earth. Midjourney didn’t look at their 100 employees and say “let’s serve a niche market.” They went after the entirety of visual creation. These companies didn’t use AI to shrink. They used small teams to think enormous.

So let me reframe the org size question entirely. It’s not “how small can we get?” It’s: “Given that every five-person team now has the capacity of a fifty-person department, how many teams do we need to pursue the mission we actually want — not the mission we settled for when headcount was expensive?”

For some companies, the honest answer will be fewer people. The coordination roles, the management layers that exist only because the org was too big to manage itself — those do go away, and they should. But for many companies — maybe most — the right answer is: keep your people, restructure them into strike teams, and go after something ten times bigger than what you’re doing today. The talent is there. The AI leverage is there. The only thing missing is a leadership team with the vision to use it.


## The talent bar transformed

There’s a hard corollary to the ambition thesis, and I won’t soften it.

If the optimal team is five exceptional people plus AI pointed at a mission that’s five to ten times larger than what you’re doing now, you cannot afford a single weak link. In a team of five, every person occupies one of your ten communication pathways. Every person’s judgment gets multiplied by AI. A floor-level contributor doesn’t just underperform — they consume a coordination slot without providing the judgment that justifies the cost. When their mediocre judgment gets amplified by AI — when they’re directing agents to produce output that is competent-looking but subtly wrong — they generate verification burden for everyone else on the team. They make the team worse, not just by contributing less, but by consuming the team’s most precious resource: the shared attention required to maintain correctness.

This is why Lovable hires former founders. This is why Steinberger could do what he did — not because he could prompt an AI, but because he could architect a system at a level where agents could execute without him reading every line. I’ve started calling this the Steinberger Threshold: the minimum level of judgment, experience, and system-level thinking required to direct a swarm of AI agents toward correct output on complex work. It’s not “prompt engineering.” Lütke calls it “context engineering” — the ability to specify a problem so completely that it’s solvable without follow-up clarification. He says it’s made him better at everything: better emails, better specs, even better at diagnosing company politics, which he attributes to forcing himself to surface “buried disagreements on assumptions that nobody surfaced.”

The hiring question has transformed. Stop asking “can this person do the current job?” Start asking “can this person be one of five whose taste and judgment will be amplified 10 to 100 times by AI, pointed at a mission ten times larger than what we’re doing today?” Because bad judgment at that amplification factor, aimed at a bigger target, isn’t a performance issue. It’s organizational damage at machine speed. And exceptional judgment at that amplification factor, aimed at the right target, is how 45 people build a billion-dollar company in eight months.


## How to find who makes the jump

I know what you’re thinking. “I have 200 people. Some of them are great. Some are solid. Some I’m not sure about. How do I figure out who belongs on a strike team?”

You don’t have to guess. You test.

The single most diagnostic thing you can do is give someone a scout mission. A real one, not a training exercise. Pick a problem your company has been ignoring — something that matters, that’s been sitting in the backlog because nobody could staff it. Give one person full AI tooling, a week, and a clear objective: come back with a working prototype, a strategic recommendation, or a first draft that a strike team could execute against. No committee. No check-ins. No approvals. Just a person, the tools, and the problem.

What you’re testing for is not AI skill. Everybody can learn to use the tools. What you’re testing for is the cluster of traits that separates someone who can direct AI agents from someone who gets directed by them:

Can they define the problem without being told how? This is Lütke’s context engineering. The person who needs a product requirements document, a Jira ticket, and a meeting to understand what they’re building is not the same person who can look at a vague business problem and decompose it into a specification tight enough that agents can execute against it. The scout mission reveals this instantly. Some people will come back in a day with something real. Others will spend the week asking clarifying questions. Both responses tell you something important.

Do they know what “right” looks like? This is taste. When an AI agent produces output, can this person look at it and immediately distinguish “correct” from “plausible”? Not at the syntax level — at the architecture level, the strategy level, the “does this actually solve the problem or does it just look like it solves the problem” level. People with taste reject subtly wrong AI output before it compounds. People without taste ship it and generate rework.

Can they hold the whole system in their head? Steinberger directed ten agents simultaneously on a complex project without reading every line of code. He could do this because he had a system-level mental model of what he was building that was strong enough to evaluate agent output at the architectural level without inspecting every detail. This is the skill that takes years to develop and that no amount of prompt engineering courses can substitute for. The scout mission reveals whether someone has it — because the person who can hold the system will produce something coherent, and the person who can’t will produce something that works in pieces but doesn’t hold together.

Do they default to action or permission? This one is cultural and it matters more than most leaders realize. In a five-person strike team, every person needs to be someone who sees a problem and starts solving it rather than someone who sees a problem and schedules a meeting about it. The scout mission tests this directly: did they build something, or did they build a plan to build something?

The uncomfortable part: the results will not distribute the way your current performance reviews predict. Some of your highest-rated people — the ones who are great at navigating your existing organizational structure, who run good meetings, who write clear status updates, who manage stakeholders skillfully — may struggle as scouts. Those skills are coordination skills. They’re valuable in a large-team structure. In a five-person strike team, they’re overhead. Meanwhile, some of your most frustrating people — the ones who chafe at process, skip meetings, build things without asking, and occasionally ship something brilliant that nobody requested — may turn out to be exactly who you need. Those people have been fighting your organizational structure for years. The strike team model is the structure they were built for.

Run ten scout missions. Give ten different people a week each. You’ll learn more about your organization’s readiness for the strike team model in ten weeks than any consulting engagement will tell you in ten months. And you’ll likely discover that you have more strike-team-ready talent than you thought — it’s just been buried under the coordination overhead of being the wrong size.

But scout missions are diagnostic. They tell you who’s ready now. The harder question is how you build the muscle across the whole organization so that more people can make the jump over time. And the answer, I think, is executive mandate.

Lütke did something at Shopify in April 2025 that looked small from the outside and was actually enormous. He required that every team prototype with AI before beginning the real build. Not “consider using AI.” Not “explore AI if it seems relevant.” Every project, every team, prototype phase, AI first. He also made AI fluency part of performance reviews and required teams to demonstrate why AI couldn’t do a task before they were allowed to request headcount for it.

The surface reading of this is “CEO pushes AI adoption.” The deeper reading is that Lütke built a systematic evaluation pipeline for his entire organization. Every time a team prototypes with AI, they generate an eval — a concrete data point on what AI can and can’t do in their specific domain, with their specific problems. Even when the AI prototype fails, the team now has a benchmark. When the next model drops — and it will, and it will be better — they have a pre-built test harness that immediately reveals what’s newly possible. Lütke isn’t just testing the AI. He’s testing his people’s ability to specify problems well enough for AI to attempt them. He’s running scout missions at organizational scale, continuously, as a default operating practice.

This is the part most leaders miss about executive mandates. The mandate isn’t about the tool. It’s about building the organizational muscle to evaluate, specify, and direct. Every forced AI prototype is a training rep for the skills that strike teams require. The person who prototypes with AI ten times and fails seven of them has still built more specification skill than the person who attended ten meetings about AI strategy. You don’t learn to direct agents by discussing it. You learn by doing it badly, seeing what breaks, and developing the taste to know why.

The mandate also solves the cultural problem that scout missions alone can’t fix. Some of your best potential strike team members won’t volunteer for a scout mission. They’ll assume it’s a trap, a test, or extra work on top of their existing responsibilities. An executive mandate that makes AI prototyping the default — not optional, not experimental, the literal first step of every project — removes the permission barrier. It says: this is how we work now. Everyone gets the reps. And the people who take to it will self-identify faster than any talent review could surface them.


## Five diagnostic questions

I’ll leave you with five questions. They’re uncomfortable. They’re also the most useful strategic exercise most leaders will do this year.

One. Pick any person on your team. If you gave them ten AI agents and complete autonomy, could they produce the output of a department? If the answer is no, the problem isn’t the AI. It’s that you’ve hired for execution capacity in a world where execution is being automated. You need people with specification capacity — the ability to define what “right” looks like clearly enough that agents can build it.

Two. How many of your teams have more than five people? For each one, write down what the sixth person adds that justifies the coordination cost. Be specific. “They help with overflow” is not a justification when AI handles overflow. “They bring a perspective nobody else has” is a justification — but only if you can name the perspective and explain why it requires a permanent team slot rather than an advisory relationship.

Three. Do you have a written constitution — not values, not a mission statement, but specific principles where a reasonable competitor would choose the opposite? If you don’t have this, you have no standard of correctness, and without a standard of correctness, AI amplifies incoherence at the speed of inference.

Four. When was the last time a single person at your company built something from zero in less than a week that previously would have required a team of ten and a quarter? If this hasn’t happened, either you don’t have the right people or you haven’t given them the tools and the permission. Both are fixable, and both are urgent.

Five. This is the big one. If you reorganized your entire company into strike teams of five and each team had the productive capacity of a fifty-person department — what mission would you pursue? Not your current mission. Not a cost-optimized version of your current mission. What would you go after if headcount were no longer the constraint? Write that down. Now compare it to what you’re actually doing. The gap between those two answers is the ambition you’re leaving on the table. And every quarter you don’t close it, someone smaller and faster is closing it for you.


## The core thesis

The thesis is simple.

The reason your days are full of meetings is not that you have too many meetings. It’s that you have too many people on too many teams, and the coordination overhead of being the wrong size generates meeting after meeting after meeting. AI made this worse, not better, because AI increased the output per person without decreasing the cost of coordination. The penalty for being oversized is now enormous and it compounds every quarter as AI gets more capable.

The structural unit of the AI era is the five-person strike team. This isn’t new — it’s the number that evolution, the military, and software engineering have converged on independently. What’s new is that AI raised the output per person by an order of magnitude, which raised the coordination cost of each additional person by the same order of magnitude. Five optimizes for the thing that actually matters: correctness. AI made volume free. Correctness is the scarce resource. Organize for scarcity.

But the part almost everyone misses: the right response to this is not to shrink. It’s to restructure into small teams and then radically expand your ambition. You didn’t get a cost reduction. You got a force multiplier. The companies that will define the next decade are not the ones cutting headcount to protect margins. They’re the ones keeping their people, reorganizing them into strike teams, and going after missions that were impossible when each person could only produce $300K of output a year and now produce $2M.

The question is no longer “how do I do the same thing with fewer people?” The question is “what becomes possible when every five-person team has the capacity of a department?” The leaders who ask that question and answer it honestly will build the defining companies of this era. The leaders who don’t will spend their time in meetings, optimizing a structure that was already obsolete, wondering why companies a fraction of their size keep eating their lunch.

The answer is in their calendar. It’s been there the whole time.
