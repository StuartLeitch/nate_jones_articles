I’ve been thinking about why the companies with the most AI training hours are often the ones with the least to show for it.

Ninety percent of companies report investing in AI. Fewer than 40% report meaningful bottom-line impact. The usual explanation is that they picked the wrong tools, or started too late, or didn’t get executive buy-in. But I keep seeing a different pattern — organizations that did everything right by the conventional playbook and still can’t convert capability into output. They bought the seats, ran the workshops, hired the consultants. And their people are still doing most of the work manually, checking boxes on AI adoption metrics while the actual leverage sits unused.

The problem isn’t adoption. It’s that the skill the economy actually needs doesn’t have a name, doesn’t have a curriculum, and doesn’t work like any workforce skill that’s come before it.

Picture a bubble. The air inside is everything AI agents can do reliably. The air outside is everything that still requires a human. The surface — that thin membrane between the two — is where the interesting work happens: deciding what to delegate, how to verify, where to intervene, when to trust. That surface is where the value concentrates. What most people miss is that when the bubble inflates, the surface area *increases*. Every capability jump creates more boundary to operate at, not less. More seams, more judgment calls, more decisions about where human attention creates value.

Every prior workforce skill — literacy, numeracy, computer literacy, coding — was a destination. You reached it, you had it, you moved on. But the skill of working at the surface of this bubble has no fixed destination, because the surface keeps expanding outward. You can’t learn it once. You can only learn to stay on it as it moves. And the infrastructure we’ve built to teach workforce skills assumes the target stands still.

That mismatch is the most expensive gap in the global workforce right now. This briefing is about what the skill actually is, how it decomposes into practicable components, and what it means for how you hire, structure teams, and allocate your own attention.

**This briefing covers:**

- **The five operations.** Frontier operations decomposes into boundary sensing, seam design, failure model maintenance, capability forecasting, and leverage calibration — five things a person does simultaneously, not sequentially.
- **Why the gap compounds.** A person who started building this skill six months ago isn’t six months ahead. She’s operating in a different technological era — and the distance is widening.
- **Who gets this and who doesn’t.** Japan, South Korea, and Germany need this capacity most and are least equipped to build it. Singapore is the outlier that proves the model.
- **Team of One vs. Team of Five.** The two organizational units emerging in frontier-capable companies — when to use each, and what connects them.
- **What to hire for.** The interview questions that identify a frontier operator, and why traditional signals are nearly useless.
- **What to do Monday.** Concrete implications for individual contributors, managers, executives, and policymakers.

The skill I’m describing has a name: **frontier operations**.

## Frontier operations

The surface of the bubble is the frontier. Working on it — sensing where it sits, structuring handoffs across it, maintaining a model of how agents fail at the current edge, forecasting where the surface expands next, deciding where your attention creates the most value — is the operation.

Not “AI literacy” — that’s knowing what a language model is and how to write a prompt, the equivalent of teaching someone the alphabet and calling them a reader. Not “prompt engineering” — that’s one technique inside one component of the practice, like calling surgery “scalpel handling.” And not the vague gesture at “human judgment” that fills every keynote about the future of work — correctly identifying that judgment matters, incorrectly assuming that naming it is the same as teaching it.

Frontier operations is the specific, practicable, assessable version of the skill everyone is pointing at and nobody is building. It has components, it develops through practice, and it degrades without maintenance. And it’s the first workforce skill in history that expires on a quarterly cycle — which means everything we know about how to teach workforce skills is wrong for this one.

It’s also structurally resistant to its own obsolescence. Every other AI-adjacent skill eventually gets absorbed into the technology itself — prompting techniques get baked into system defaults, integration patterns get productized. But frontier operations can’t be automated, because the surface is defined by what the interior can’t yet reach. When a task migrates inside the bubble, the surface expands outward, and the person who operates at the surface moves with it.


## The five operations

Frontier operations decomposes into five things a person does simultaneously — the way surgery integrates anatomy, dexterity, decision-making, and team coordination into something that none of those components fully describes.


### 1. Boundary sensing

The ability to maintain accurate, up-to-date operational intuition about where the human-agent boundary sits for a given domain.

None of this is static knowledge. It updates with every model release, every capability jump, every subtle shift in how agents handle long context or tool use. Sonnet 4.5 scored below 20% on long-context retrieval. Opus 4.6 scores 76% on the same benchmark at one million tokens. A person who calibrated her boundary sense against last quarter’s model and hasn’t updated is now either over-trusting or under-using the current model — and both errors are expensive.

The skill is maintaining the calibration, not having it once.

What this looks like in practice:

A product manager with good boundary sensing knows, right now, that an agent can draft a credible competitive analysis but will miss the political dynamics between two executives it’s never observed. She doesn’t hand it the whole analysis. She hands it the market sizing and feature comparison — tasks now safely inside the bubble — and reserves the stakeholder-dynamics section for herself. Last quarter she was doing the feature comparison manually. She updated.

A financial analyst knows that an agent can build a DCF model from clean inputs and pull comps across a sector, but will silently mishandle a non-standard revenue-recognition policy buried in a footnote on page 47 of a 10-K. She lets the agent run the model, then checks the assumptions — specifically the revenue-recognition treatment — rather than re-running the whole spreadsheet herself.

A marketing director knows that an agent produces solid first-draft campaign copy and can A/B test headline variants, but will generate brand-voice copy that drifts subtly off-tone after the third or fourth revision. She uses the agent for ideation and first drafts, edits for voice herself, and has stopped asking it to iterate past V2.

What bad looks like: She either trusts everything (gets burned by hallucinations she didn’t catch) or trusts nothing (does everything manually while wondering why she pays for AI tools). Most commonly: she calibrated once, six months ago, and hasn’t noticed that the boundary moved.


### 2. Seam design

The ability to structure work so that transitions between human and agent phases are clean, verifiable, and recoverable.

This is an architectural skill. It’s closer to how a good engineering manager thinks about system boundaries than to how an individual contributor thinks about their tasks. The person doing seam design is asking: if I break this project into seven phases, which three are fully agent-executable, which two need human-in-the-loop, and which two are still irreducibly human? What artifacts pass between each phase? What do I need to see at each transition to know things are on track?

The reason this is a distinct skill and not just project management is that the answer changes as capabilities shift. The seam that was in the right place last quarter is in the wrong place this quarter. The skill isn’t in the design. It’s in the redesign.

What this looks like in practice:

A software engineering lead at a company like Rakuten structures the handoff so that ticket triage and work routing go to the agent, architectural decisions stay with the humans, and the boundary between those two is defined by specific artifacts — the content of the ticket, the structure of the codebase, the org chart — and specific verification checks at each seam. Those seams are why it works. Without them, you get either end-to-end agent runs with a prayer, or humans manually reviewing things the agent handles better than they do.

A consulting engagement manager breaks a strategy project into research (agent-led, with human-defined scope), synthesis (human-led, with agent-generated first-pass frameworks), and client presentation (human-led, with agent-generated slide drafts). The seam between research and synthesis is a structured deliverable: a fact base with source citations the human can spot-check in ten minutes. Six months ago that seam included manual fact verification on every data point. The agent’s citation accuracy improved. She moved the seam.

A newsroom editor designs the workflow so that an agent does initial source aggregation and fact-pattern detection across wire services, a junior reporter verifies and adds original sourcing, and a senior editor shapes the narrative. The artifact that passes from agent to reporter is a structured brief — not a draft article — because the seam is cleaner when the human isn’t editing agent prose but building on agent research.


### 3. Failure model maintenance

The ability to maintain an accurate, current mental model of how agents fail — not that they fail, but the specific texture and shape of failure at the current capability level.

Early large language models failed obviously. Garbled text, wrong facts, incoherent reasoning — the kind of errors anyone could catch. Current frontier models fail subtly: correct-sounding analysis built on a misunderstood premise. Plausible code that handles the happy path perfectly and silently breaks on edge cases. Research summaries that are 95% accurate with the remaining 5% confidently fabricated in a way that’s indistinguishable from the accurate parts unless you already know the domain.

The skill is not “be skeptical of AI output.” That’s necessary but useless — it’s like saying the skill of surgery is “be careful.” The skill is maintaining a differentiated failure model: knowing that for Task Type A, the agent’s failure mode is X and here’s how to check for it efficiently, while for Task Type B, the failure mode is Y and here’s a different check.

What this looks like in practice:

A corporate counsel knows that an agent reviewing contracts will catch boilerplate issues — missing indemnification clauses, non-standard termination language — with near-perfect accuracy, but will miss the interaction between a specific liability cap in Section 7 and a carve-out buried in an exhibit. Her failure model says: trust the boilerplate scan, manually review cross-references between liability provisions and exhibits. That’s a different check than “read the whole thing again,” and it takes a quarter of the time.

A data scientist knows that an agent generating Python for data analysis handles pandas transformations and standard statistical tests reliably but produces plausible-looking nonsense when the data has messy edge cases — mixed date formats, implicit nulls encoded as -999, columns that changed meaning mid-dataset. Her failure model says: verify the data-cleaning steps and the assumptions about column semantics; trust the downstream analysis if the cleaning is right.

A recruiter knows the agent writes solid initial outreach and can synthesize a candidate’s background from public sources, but consistently overweights credential signals and misses non-obvious career-transition narratives. Her failure model says: trust the factual summary, rewrite the value proposition, and never let the agent rank candidates without human override on non-traditional backgrounds.

What bad looks like: She either applies the same generic skepticism to everything — inefficient, catches some things, misses others — or has memorized failure patterns from six months ago that no longer match reality.


### 4. Capability forecasting

The ability to make reasonable short-term predictions about where the boundary will move next, and to invest learning and workflow development accordingly.

This is not about predicting the future of AI. Nobody can do that reliably. It’s about reading trajectory well enough to make sensible six-to-twelve-month bets about what will become agent territory.

Think of it as reading swells. A surfer doesn’t predict exactly what the next wave will look like. But a good surfer reads the ocean, understands how the floor shapes waves at this particular break, and positions herself where the next rideable wave is most likely to form. The skill is probabilistic positioning, not prediction.

What this looks like in practice:

A person with good capability forecasting in early 2025 would have looked at the trajectory of coding agents — thirty minutes of sustained autonomy, then seven hours at Rakuten, then two full weeks for a C compiler — and started investing in code review and specification skills rather than raw coding. She would not have bet heavily on any specific tool or platform, because she understood that the capability layer commoditizes while the operational skill layer compounds.

A supply chain manager watching agents get better at demand forecasting and inventory optimization would start investing now in exception-handling workflows — the judgment calls that happen when the forecast is wrong or when real-world disruptions break the model. That’s where the boundary is heading. The person who waits until agents fully own baseline forecasting to start building exception-handling skill is already behind.

A UX researcher watching agents get better at survey design and qualitative coding would start investing in interpretive synthesis — the skill of turning coded data into product insight that changes a roadmap. The coding is migrating inside the bubble. The “so what” is where the new surface will be.

What bad looks like: She either chases every new tool — exhausting, no compound returns — or ignores developments until forced to catch up. Most commonly: she invests heavily in learning one specific platform, then that platform’s advantage evaporates when the next model ships and she’s back to zero.


### 5. Leverage calibration

The ability to make high-quality decisions about where to spend human attention — the scarcest resource in an agent-rich environment.

As agent capabilities increase, the bottleneck shifts from “getting things done” to “knowing which things are worth a human’s attention.” McKinsey’s framework describes two to five humans supervising 50 to 100 specialized agents running an end-to-end process. That ratio makes the math of attention brutally clear. If you have a hundred streams of agent output and eight hours a day, you cannot review everything at the same depth. The skill is triaging your own attention in real time.

What this looks like in practice:

An engineering manager overseeing agent-assisted development across five teams develops a hierarchical attention allocation: most agent-generated code flows through automated test suites and linting. A smaller subset — changes touching authentication, billing, or data pipelines — gets flagged for human code review. Only architectural decisions and cross-system changes get deep human engagement. She recalibrates these thresholds monthly, because the agents keep getting better at the routine tier and she keeps finding new categories that belong in the middle tier.

A portfolio manager running agent-assisted research on 200 equities lets the agent generate daily summaries and flag anomalies. She reads the anomaly flags. She deep-dives on three to five names per day. She never reads the summaries for positions that are small, liquid, and within parameters. The leverage isn’t in the research. It’s in knowing which research to ignore.

A head of customer success overseeing agent-handled support tickets reviews escalations and a random sample of resolved tickets. She doesn’t review the resolution of routine password resets. She does review every ticket where the agent accessed account modification tools. The threshold is calibrated to risk, not volume — and she adjusts it whenever the agent’s tool-use capabilities change.

What bad looks like: She either reviews everything at the same depth — creating a bottleneck, burning out, getting diminishing returns on her attention — or reviews nothing, until a catastrophic failure surfaces.


## Why these aren’t a checklist

These five operations are not sequential steps. They’re simultaneous, integrated, and continuous — the way driving involves steering, speed management, route awareness, hazard perception, and vehicle feel all running at the same time.

At any given moment, a person operating at the frontier is sensing the current boundary, designing seams around it, verifying against an updated failure model, making bets about where the boundary will move, and allocating attention across the whole system. The integration is what makes this a practice and not a curriculum. You can teach each component in isolation. But a person who’s good at all five individually and can’t run them simultaneously isn’t operating at the frontier — she’s thinking about operating at the frontier.

And the gap compounds. A person who develops frontier operations skill six months earlier than her peer doesn’t just have a six-month head start. She has six months of updated calibration that her peer hasn’t built. And because capabilities are accelerating, the distance between “calibrated” and “uncalibrated” grows wider with every model release. The person whose boundary sense was current in February and the person whose boundary sense was last updated in August aren’t six months apart. They’re operating in different technological eras.


## Who gets this and who doesn’t

This is the mechanism behind the leverage numbers that keep appearing in production deployments. Rakuten’s engineers running five parallel tasks — four delegated to Claude, one human-led. Cursor hitting $100M ARR with roughly 20 people. Lovable reaching $100M ARR with roughly 45 employees. The gap between those numbers and the traditional SaaS benchmark isn’t explained by better tools alone. It’s explained by people who have developed the operational practice to convert those tools into output.

The question isn’t which economies build the models or have the most compute — it’s which economies can field the largest number of workers who are good at operating at the frontier. The models are commoditizing and the compute is being built everywhere. What’s scarce is the human capacity to convert those inputs into economic output — and that conversion happens at the human-agent boundary, nowhere else.

The countries that need frontier operations capacity most urgently are largely the ones least equipped to build it. Japan, South Korea, and Germany share a common profile: aging populations, shrinking workforces, expensive labor, and education systems built for fixed-target skills. Japan’s working-age population is projected to shrink to 60% of its 2000 level by 2050. South Korea became a “super-aged” society at the end of 2024 — one of the fastest demographic transitions in recorded history. Germany has over 600,000 unfilled vacancies. All three need every remaining worker to have maximum leverage over their output. All three have training infrastructure precisely optimized for the wrong kind of skill.

Singapore is the outlier that proves the point. Budget 2026 treats workforce development as a national security function: a new National AI Council chaired by the Prime Minister, 400% tax deductions on AI spending for businesses, six months of free access to premium AI tools for anyone completing selected training courses. Singapore isn’t just funding courses. It’s funding practice cycles — making it cheaper for workers to actually use AI tools on real work, observe results, update their models, and try again. The tax incentives increase the opportunity for calibration reps, not just classroom hours.

The US is wildly bifurcated. The top 5% of the workforce is arguably the most frontier-capable on earth. The other 95% are getting “AI literacy” training that teaches them to use ChatGPT, which is approximately as useful as teaching someone to use a typewriter in 1995.


## What leaders and policymakers should actually do

The structural insight is simple: you cannot teach a moving-target skill with a fixed-target method. The only training method that works for frontier operations is one where the practitioner is repeatedly placed in situations where she has to sense the boundary, design seams, verify against real failure modes, forecast capability shifts, and allocate attention — and where the conditions keep changing.

Build practice environments, not courseware. What flight simulation is to aviation, frontier operations simulation should be to AI-era work. Practice environments where agents have different capability levels, where failure modes are realistic and varied, where the rules change periodically so practitioners have to recalibrate rather than memorize. Ninety percent of companies report investing in AI, but fewer than 40% report meaningful bottom-line impact. The gap exists because most organizations apply AI to individual tasks rather than redesigning workflows — and that skill can only be built through practice.

Measure calibration, not knowledge. The right assessment isn’t “can you write a good prompt?” It’s “given a task and an agent at capability level X, can you accurately predict where the agent will succeed, where it will fail, and how to structure the work accordingly?” Columbia’s study on Microsoft Copilot adoption found that seven in ten participants ignored onboarding videos, relying instead on trial and error and social learning from peers. The workforce is already telling us what works.

The speed of skill development is a function of how many calibration cycles a person gets per unit of time — not how many hours of training she completes. A person who completes a 40-hour AI course and then returns to a workplace where she never touches an AI tool has zero calibration cycles. A person who skips the course but delegates ten real tasks a day to an agent and evaluates the output has hundreds.

Create explicit roles for frontier operations. The skill won’t develop if it’s just an undifferentiated part of everyone’s job. Organizations need people whose specific function is to operate at the boundary — to maintain the failure models, update the verification protocols, redesign workflows when capabilities shift. Call them AI operations leads, delegation architects, frontier engineers. The title matters less than the recognition that this is a distinct function, not a hobby someone does between their actual responsibilities.


## How to hire and structure for this

The org chart built for the pre-agent era assumes that output scales with headcount. More people, more output. The frontier operations era inverts this. Output scales with leverage — and leverage scales with how well a small number of humans operate at the boundary. The organizational unit that matters isn’t the department. It’s the pod.

Two structures are emerging, and the companies that figure out how to compose them will outperform everyone else.

The Team of One. A single person with strong frontier operations skill, running multiple agent workflows across a domain. She does the boundary sensing, designs her own seams, maintains her own failure models, and calibrates her own attention. Her output looks like what a five-to-ten-person team produced two years ago — not because she works harder, but because she delegates continuously and verifies intelligently. This is the Cursor model, the Lovable model, the “one person with high leverage” archetype. It works when the domain is well-understood, the feedback loops are tight, and the work is primarily execution against a known pattern.

A strong Team of One in marketing runs campaign strategy, content production, performance analysis, and creative iteration — with agents handling first-draft copy, audience segmentation, A/B test design, and reporting dashboards. She’s not managing people. She’s managing workflows. Her job is knowing where the agent handles it and where she steps in, and updating that map weekly.

A strong Team of One in software engineering owns a full feature vertical — spec, implementation, testing, deployment. Agents handle boilerplate code, test generation, documentation, and routine bug triage. She handles architectural decisions, edge-case reasoning, and the judgment calls about what to ship and what to hold. Two years ago this was a team of six with a product manager attached.

The Team of Five. A small pod — typically one person with deep frontier operations skill, two to three people with developing skill, and one to two specialists whose domain expertise is irreplaceable but whose operational skill is still building. The frontier operator sets the seams, maintains the failure models, and calibrates the attention allocation for the whole pod. The others execute within those structures, developing their own frontier intuition through practice. This is closer to how a surgical team works: one lead who sees the whole field, supported by people with complementary skill executing in defined roles.

A Team of Five in a consulting practice might be one engagement lead running the overall human-agent workflow architecture, two associates executing agent-assisted research and analysis within seams the lead defined, one domain specialist who brings client-industry depth the agents can’t match, and one person focused on client delivery and narrative. The pod handles what used to require a twelve-to-fifteen-person engagement team — not because the work shrank, but because the seams are well-designed and the agent leverage is real.

A Team of Five in product development might be one frontier operator who owns the human-agent workflow across the whole product surface, two engineers executing agent-assisted development within that architecture, one designer running agent-assisted prototyping and user research, and one data scientist managing the analytics pipeline. They ship at the pace of a twenty-person team because the operator keeps the seams current and the failure models calibrated, and the rest of the pod has enough frontier skill to execute without constant supervision.

How it ladders up. The composition question for leaders isn’t “how many people do I need?” It’s “how many Teams of One and how many Teams of Five — and what connects them?”

Teams of One are cheap, fast, and fragile. They’re cheap because one person with leverage can cover enormous ground. They’re fast because there’s no coordination overhead. They’re fragile because if that person leaves, gets sick, or falls behind on calibration, there’s no backup — and the institutional knowledge of where the seams are and how the agents fail walks out the door with her.

Teams of Five are more expensive, slightly slower, but resilient. The frontier skill is distributed across the pod. The lead’s calibration is partially transferred to the team through shared practice. If one person leaves, the remaining pod can maintain operations while someone new develops.

The right org structure for most companies is a lattice: Teams of One handling well-understood, lower-risk domains where speed matters most, and Teams of Five handling complex, high-stakes, or cross-functional domains where resilience matters most. A frontier operations lead — or a small team of them — sits above the lattice, maintaining the macro-level boundary sense, spotting when a Team of One’s domain has gotten complex enough to warrant a pod, and recognizing when a Team of Five’s domain has become routine enough that a solo operator can handle it.

The hardest part of this isn’t the org design. It’s identifying who can actually operate at the frontier. Traditional hiring signals — credentials, years of experience, tool proficiency — are nearly useless here. A person with ten years of experience and no boundary sense is less valuable than a person with two years and sharp calibration.

What to look for instead: Does this person track where agents succeed and fail in her domain? Can she articulate, specifically, what an agent handles well today and where it breaks? When you describe a new capability, does she immediately start redesigning her workflow, or does she file it under “interesting”? Does she have a failure model — not generic skepticism, but a differentiated understanding of how agents fail on which tasks? Can she forecast where the boundary is heading in her domain and explain why?

The person who answers those questions well is your frontier operator. The person who answers them with “I’m really good at prompting” is not.


## What this means for you

If you’re an individual contributor: Start tracking where your boundary sense is wrong. Every time an agent surprises you — produces something better than you expected, or fails in a way you didn’t predict — that’s data. The surprise is the signal. Collect those signals deliberately. The person who logs “I thought Claude couldn’t do X, but it can” and “I assumed Claude would handle Y, but it broke on the edge case where…” is building the most valuable professional asset in the economy: a current, differentiated understanding of where human attention creates value and where it doesn’t.

**If you manage people:** Look at how your team allocates attention across agent-assisted work. Are they reviewing everything at the same depth? That’s a bottleneck masquerading as diligence. Are they reviewing nothing? That’s negligence dressed up as trust. The right answer is differentiated — deep attention on high-stakes, novel, or failure-prone work; light touch on routine, well-understood, low-risk work; automated checks on everything else. If your team can’t articulate which is which and why, that’s the gap.

**If you run an organization:** The question isn’t “are we using AI?” It’s “do we have people whose job it is to know where the boundary is and redesign our workflows when it moves?” If the answer is no — if frontier operations is something you expect to emerge organically from people’s day jobs — you are leaving the most consequential organizational capability of the decade to chance.

**If you set policy:** Stop building curricula. Start building flight simulators. The workforce development infrastructure that matters for the next ten years is not more courses, more certifications, more “AI literacy” frameworks. It’s practice environments that expose workers to realistic agent capabilities, realistic failure modes, and realistic conditions that change — so that calibration becomes a reflex, not a credential.


## The audit

Ten questions. If you answer “no” to more than three, you have a frontier operations gap that is costing you real output.

1. Can you name, specifically, three tasks your team delegated to agents last quarter that they didn’t delegate the quarter before?
2. When the last major model update shipped, did anyone on your team formally reassess which workflows to change — or did everyone keep doing what they were doing?
3. Do you have at least one person whose explicit responsibility includes knowing where agents fail in your domain — not generically, but by task type?
4. Can your team articulate the difference between tasks where they trust agent output without review and tasks where they always verify — and explain why the line is where it is?
5. Has anyone on your team redesigned a workflow handoff between human and agent work in the last 90 days?
6. When an agent produces something better than expected or fails in an unexpected way, does your team have a mechanism for capturing that signal — or does it just get absorbed and forgotten?
7. Is your team’s review depth differentiated by risk, or does everyone check everything at the same depth?
8. Can anyone on your team make a credible six-month forecast about which of their current tasks will migrate to agents — and say what they’re investing in to stay ahead of that shift?
9. Do you have explicit roles for frontier operations, or is it something you expect to emerge from people’s day jobs?
10. If your strongest frontier operator left tomorrow, could the rest of the team maintain the current level of agent-assisted output — or would it collapse?

If question 10 gave you pause, reread the section on Teams of One versus Teams of Five.


## The bubble doesn’t stop

In November 2025, the state of the art was one thing. By February 2026, it was a different thing — a generational leap compressed into a single quarter. Context windows expanded five-fold and retrieval accuracy went from one in five to three in four. Agent teams shipped as a production feature. A C compiler was built by sixteen agents working for two weeks with minimal human intervention.

That was one quarter. The next quarter will bring another shift. Every quarter of delay compounds — not linearly, but geometrically. The person who started building calibration in November has three months of updated operational intuition that the person starting now doesn’t have. The first-mover advantage in frontier operations isn’t about being early to a fixed destination. It’s about having more reps at a skill that requires constant reps to maintain.

The bubble is inflating. The surface is expanding. And the skill that matters isn’t reaching the surface — it’s staying on it as it moves.

Welcome to the frontier. It’s expanding — and so should you.
