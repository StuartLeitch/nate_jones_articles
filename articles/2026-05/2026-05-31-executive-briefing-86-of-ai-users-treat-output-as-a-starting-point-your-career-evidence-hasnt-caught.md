A reader wrote in with a job search problem that has been sitting with me. They had been laid off, and the work they were proudest of was the work they could not show.

They had made decisions with limited information and kept a team moving through a quarter that could have gone sideways. They had understood a messy system better than anyone else in the room. In interviews, they had to tell those stories over and over to people who were skeptical by default.

I’ve been thinking about why that problem keeps getting worse.

AI did not break human judgment. It broke the signal that judgment used to leave behind. A polished memo no longer proves you understood the business. A clean prototype no longer proves you understood the user. Everyone can look productive now. The hard part is seeing who actually understood the work.

And this runs in both directions. If you run an organization, your evaluation systems are losing signal. If you are the talent — especially if you sit far from the execution layer — the evidence problem is worse. You did not write the code. You did not design the screen. You made the call that kept the company from spending millions on the wrong bet, and there is no artifact for that.

The thinking layer has to travel with the work.

The evidence problem I am describing hits hardest at the executive level, because the work is almost entirely judgment — portfolio bets, org design, market timing, decisions that shaped the company for years. None of that ships as a work sample. So this briefing talks to you as someone who evaluates others, but also as someone who may be facing the same problem from the other side.

## The old evidence stopped working

Valuable work has always been hard to see. Not the output, but what sat behind it: the decision not to build something, the meeting where you prevented six weeks of wasted effort, the technical tradeoff you made because the obvious solution would have created a maintenance problem later. The moment you realized the team was solving the wrong problem and changed the shape of the work.

Before AI, output still carried some signal. If someone shipped the roadmap, wrote the strategy doc, delivered the analysis, or kept the team moving, the artifact suggested something about the person behind it. Production was hard enough that the finished work told part of the story.

AI weakens that link.

Microsoft’s 2026 Work Trend Index says 86 percent of AI users treat AI output as a starting point, not a final answer. 58 percent are producing work they could not have produced a year earlier. Among the advanced users Microsoft calls frontier professionals, that number rises to 80 percent.

A tidy project plan does not prove you knew which tradeoffs mattered. A sharp resume does not prove very much at all.

That does not make human judgment less valuable. It makes human judgment harder to see.

The answer is not “build more projects.” The world does not need another pile of AI-assisted demos with no context attached. A hiring manager, a promotion committee, or a potential collaborator needs more than proof that you can produce an artifact. They need proof that you can think inside a real situation.

The work sample is no longer enough.


## The whiteboard discipline

I believe the AI age is the age of whiteboards.

If I want to know whether someone really understands a problem, I want to see them at a whiteboard with someone strong enough to push them. The problem should be real. The room should be serious. The person should have to draw what they know, name what they do not know, explain where the system is fragile, and say which risk they would take.

Then the other person should push back.

A whiteboard conversation is valuable because it turns private judgment into visible work before the work gets cleaned up. The person has to think in the room. They have to hold the situation in their head, respond to pressure, update when they learn something, and show where their confidence ends.

That live reasoning is the evidence.

A good whiteboard conversation also shows how someone learns. Do they get defensive when challenged? Do they update too quickly just to please the room? Do they hold a useful line when the argument is sound? Do they separate facts from assumptions? Do they know when they are out of their depth?

We are not looking for perfect confidence or perfect recall. We are looking for judgment under pressure.

The discipline scales with the level. At the IC level, the whiteboard is about a system: how it works, where it breaks, which fix to choose. At the executive level, the whiteboard is about a portfolio of bets. Explaining to a board why you killed a product line that was still generating revenue to fund something that will not show returns for 18 months. Defending a reorg when half your direct reports think the old structure was fine and the other half are waiting to see who wins. The stakes are higher, the information is more ambiguous, and the consequences of being wrong are measured in years, not sprints. That kind of pressure is harder to rehearse and harder to fake.

The same discipline works when there is no physical board. A shared doc with the four elements at the top. A digital whiteboard where the reasoning stays inspectable. An RFC that preserves the rejected options. A Loom where someone walks through a real problem and names the risks out loud. The format matters less than the discipline: make the reasoning visible while it is still alive.


## Four questions that make judgment portable

The structure is simple. For any problem, any decision, any piece of work where judgment mattered, write down four things.

**Situation.** What is happening? Who is involved? What constraints matter? What facts do we have, and what facts are missing? Where is the pressure coming from? Why is this hard?

Context is where judgment begins. Most people under-explain their own situations. They say “I led the migration” or “I managed the roadmap” or “I improved the process.” Work only becomes legible when the reader understands what you were operating inside.

A weak work sample says: “I led a cross-functional project to improve onboarding.”

A stronger version says: “I inherited an onboarding project that had already missed two deadlines. Sales wanted more customization, support wanted fewer manual steps, and engineering was worried the existing workflow would not scale. The hardest part was not writing the new process. It was deciding which group’s pain mattered most for the next release.”

Now the reader can see the work.

**Decision.** What are the plausible paths? Which one would you take? Which ones would you reject? What would make you change your mind?

Good work usually involves rejecting plausible options. The rejected options matter because they show what you understood and refused to hand-wave.

**Risk.** What could go wrong? What risk are you willing to take? What risk are you consciously accepting because the alternative is worse?

Risk is one of the clearest ways to make invisible work visible. A lot of good judgment looks like nothing happened. The bad launch did not happen. The team did not spend two months building the wrong feature. The model output did not go into production without review.

Name the risk. Prevented losses count.

**Change.** If we make this decision, what gets clearer? What gets safer? What gets faster? What work stops? What decision stops being relitigated? What does the team understand after the conversation that it did not understand before?

This keeps the exercise from becoming a diary. The point is to connect judgment to a change in the work.


## What judgment artifacts look like in practice

A division lead can write: “Three business units were selling overlapping products to the same enterprise customers. Each unit had its own engineering team, its own roadmap, and its own sales motion. Customers were getting three different pitches from the same company. I consolidated into two units, killed the smallest product line, and moved its engineering team onto the platform that served the largest customer base. The decision cost us roughly $3M in annual revenue from the killed product. Within two quarters, the remaining teams had reduced customer confusion, shortened the sales cycle, and the engineers who moved over shipped the integration work the old structure never had capacity to prioritize.”

Compare that to “restructured the product organization.”

An engineering manager can write: “I took over a team that was shipping more slowly every quarter. The obvious diagnosis was engineering velocity, but the real issue was decision churn. Product priorities changed weekly, support escalations bypassed planning, and engineers were starting work before requirements stabilized. I introduced a weekly decision review with three categories: committed, under discussion, and explicitly deferred. Within six weeks, the team had fewer surprise escalations and engineers were able to finish work without constant resets.”

That tells me more than “improved team velocity.”

From a PM: “We had pressure to add an AI summarization feature because competitors had launched similar functionality. After reviewing support tickets and customer calls, I found that users did not primarily need summaries. They needed confidence that the next action was correct. I recommended a narrower workflow that extracted decisions, owners, and deadlines instead of producing a general summary. The result was less impressive in a demo but more useful in the weekly operating rhythm.”

More useful than “launched AI feature.”

For a senior IC, the same structure applies: “The first proposal used a more flexible event-driven architecture. I pushed for a simpler synchronous path because the team had limited observability and no one owned the operational burden of the more complex design. We accepted less flexibility in exchange for debuggability. Six months later, when the workflow had to change, the simpler system was still easy enough for a new engineer to modify.”

That tells me more than “designed scalable architecture.”

From an operator: “The company had a recurring problem where leadership meetings generated action items but no decisions. I rebuilt the meeting output around decision rights: who decides, what evidence is needed, what date the decision is due, and what happens if no decision is made. The visible artifact was a simple tracker. The actual work was changing the meeting from status-sharing to decision production.”

That shows the operating cadence rather than merely naming it.

A sanitized example makes this concrete. A product-operations lead I worked with had done the invisible part of a hard roadmap reset: she separated customer noise from a real retention problem, showed which metric was misleading, and got the team to kill a feature that would have consumed a quarter of engineering time. None of that could be posted as a company case study. But the judgment artifact was shareable: the situation, the false signal, the decision, the risk, and the business consequence, with customer names, revenue figures, roadmap details, and internal screenshots removed. The artifact did not reveal the company’s private work. It revealed her judgment.


## Your career evidence is thinner than you think

If you are a senior leader looking for your next role, this problem hits harder, not softer.

The standard career advice tells people to build a portfolio. Show what you shipped. Put your work online. That advice was always incomplete for judgment-heavy roles, and it gets worse the further you are from the execution layer.

A VP of product did not write the PRD. A CTO did not commit the code. A COO did not build the dashboard. What they did was make portfolio-level calls: which bets to take, which to kill, how to structure the organization so the right decisions got made at the right level. The work is real. It is often the most consequential work in the company. And almost none of it leaves an artifact you can share.

You cannot publish the board deck or the M&A analysis. You cannot put the internal reorg plan in a public portfolio or share the Slack thread where you told the CEO the strategy was wrong and walked through the evidence until they agreed.

A fractional executive has an even harder version of this problem. The judgment was real, spread across three or four clients in the last year, all of it under NDA, none of it producing artifacts they own. The evidence is locked behind doors they no longer have keys to.

An entrepreneur who shut down a company or pivoted a business made some of the hardest calls of their career. The visible result looks like a failure on a resume even when the judgment was sound. They saw the market shifting, cut losses before the burn rate became fatal, and preserved enough capital and team to try again. The decision was the work. The outcome obscures it.

But you can make the judgment structure portable.

Pick three real moments from the last two years where your judgment changed an outcome. Not necessarily the biggest projects. The moments where the decision was hard, the alternatives were plausible, and the outcome would have been meaningfully different without your involvement. For each one, rebuild the whiteboard. Write one page.

Keep it sanitized. Remove names, confidential numbers, internal links, roadmap details, and anything your employer would reasonably consider private. Change identifying details. Turn exact figures into ranges or remove them entirely. Preserve the shape of the decision. What you are sharing is not the company’s private information. You are sharing the judgment structure: the situation, the options, the risk, and the consequence.

The first months in a new role are one of the best places to start. If judgment is the valuable work, then starting strong means forming a point of view early and letting people see how that point of view improves. Ask for a whiteboard session with someone who understands the domain deeply. Walk through what you think the customer problem is so far. Name where you think the team is over-weighting. Identify the risk you want to validate before committing.

Then let the other person push. If they correct you, write it down. If they expose a weak assumption, do not defend the assumption just because it was yours. Serious work requires a point of view. What matters is whether the opinion is earned: grounded in observation, evidence, and the discipline to update when the facts change.


## What this changes about evaluation

If you evaluate people, this should change what you ask for.

Do not stop at “What did you build?” Ask what the person understood that other people were missing. When you ask about the result, ask what tradeoff they made and what would have gone wrong if they had chosen differently. If AI was involved, the interesting question is not whether they used it. The interesting question is where it helped, where it failed, and how they knew.

One interview question I would use right now: tell me about a decision you made in the last six months that an outsider would have made differently, and walk me through your reasoning.

If possible, make it a whiteboard conversation. Let them draw the situation, mark the decision, name the risk, tell you what changed. Then challenge one part and watch what happens.

That question gets past the polished artifact. It asks for the work loop: messy context, point of view, evidence, decision, risk, outcome, and revision.

Inside companies, managers should use the same frame in performance reviews and promotion packets. One of the worst effects of AI at work is that it can make shallow productivity look impressive. The person generating the most documents may look like the person doing the most work. The person posting the most AI experiments in Slack may look like the most AI-fluent employee.

Maybe they are. Maybe they are not.

The way to tell is to look for the thinking. Can they explain why the work mattered and what assumptions they were carrying? Can they name the tradeoffs they rejected? Where did AI help, where was it wrong, and how did they know? What changed for the business, user, system, or team?

If they can, you are looking at judgment.


## The work needs help speaking

Many people who are good at work are bad at showing it. They assume the work will speak for itself. Sometimes it used to. More often, it did not. In the AI era, it definitely will not.

The work needs help speaking. And the way to help it is not to make the output shinier. It is to make the judgment behind it visible.

The whiteboard reveals judgment in the room. But judgment needs somewhere to live after the room clears. If you are looking for your next role, your best thinking is trapped inside company-owned systems you will lose access to the day you leave. The Slack threads, the internal docs, the decision logs, the meeting notes where you changed the shape of the work. All of it disappears when your badge stops working. If you are hiring, you are making high-stakes decisions based on a resume, a LinkedIn profile, and an hour of polished interviewing. You have no way to see how someone actually reasons until they are already on the team.

Block 45 minutes this Friday afternoon. Pick the single moment in the last six months where your judgment changed an outcome. Write it as 250 words against the four questions: situation, decision, risk, change. Put it in your TalentBoard profile, your personal site, or the packet you use when you interview. Then tell three people in your network where it lives.

Most people do not have this asset today: proof that they understood the work.
