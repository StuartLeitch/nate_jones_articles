## What “a 2026 pace” actually means

The unit of work changed, and most people haven’t noticed because the chat box still looks the same.

In 2023 and 2024, working with AI meant asking. You typed into a box, a model wrote back, you judged the answer. That was real value, and it trained an entire generation of habits. And those habits are now the ceiling. Because in 2026, the people moving fast aren’t asking better questions. They’re handing bounded jobs to a machine that’s wired into their actual stuff, getting back finished artifacts with proof attached, and running several lanes of that at once. That shift went from the prompt to the run: a goal, a boundary, tools, artifacts, receipts.

That sentence can sound abstract until you put it against a normal day.

A normal AI day for most people still looks like this: a question comes up, you open a model, you paste context, you ask for help, you copy the answer back into your real work, and then you personally carry the result across the last mile. You rename the file. You check the source. You make the edit. You send the email. You update the sheet. You remember what worked last time. The model helped, but the loop still lives in your head and hands.

A Codex day looks different. You open the folder, the repo, the page, the spreadsheet, the transcript, the draft, the dashboard, whatever the real work is, and you give the agent a bounded job inside that environment. Map this folder. Compare these drafts. Find the broken link. Pull action items from this call. Build the page and check it in the browser. Run the test. Update the guide. Leave the evidence in the side panel. Then the next run starts from the artifact, not from your memory of what you meant.

That is the difference. Not a better answer. A tighter loop.

If your AI use is still paste-into-a-box, you are working at a 2023 pace with 2026 tools. That’s the entire thing. And Codex is the fastest on-ramp I know to the new unit of work, because of three properties that have nothing to do with how smart the model is.

**It connects to your stuff.** Codex is remarkably good at discovering connectors and wiring itself into the places your work actually lives: data sources, tools, APIs, the systems you already use. And the skills convention means the wiring is done once and stays done: a skill is a small file that carries the connection: which endpoint, what the call looks like, where the key lives, the gotchas you already paid for. And every future session inherits it. You stop re-introducing the model to your life every morning.

**It gives the model hands and feet.** Not metaphorically. There’s a real browser inside the app where it navigates and builds pages while you watch. There’s a terminal where it runs real commands. It reads and writes real files. It can even drive desktop software that has a scripting API — the last stage of my video pipeline is Codex operating the actual editor. This is the part that still gets me after months of daily use: the model isn’t describing work anymore. It’s doing the work, on the machine, in front of you.

**It doesn’t give up.** Codex is determined in a way that took me a while to fully trust. Give it a goal with a real finish line and it pursues that goal across steps, across sessions, across your absences. Goal mode has been standard in the app since May. The craft is in the finish line: “improve the test coverage” is a wish, but “every module in src/lib has tests, the suite passes, report saved to docs/coverage.md” is a goal it can check itself against. There are mornings where the first useful things at my desk are things Codex finished before I sat down.

Add the three together, because the sum is the whole story. A model that’s connected to your sources, can operate the machine, and stays on a goal isn’t a chatbot. **Chat is the model sitting beside your work. Codex puts the model in the middle of it.**

And that’s why it’s my daily driver — not loyalty, utility. It ships pages on my site. It runs the first pass on my video edits. It turns voice memos I record on walks into drafted, published posts. It keeps a live dashboard of what my AI usage actually costs. It does the dozen small jobs a week I used to do by hand or, more honestly, used to not do at all. I write about every tool in this space, I published a whole piece this week on the different habits Claude and Codex train, and I’ll tell you the moment something displaces it. Nothing has.

## The mistake to avoid: learning Codex like software

The obvious way to approach Codex is also the wrong one: open the app, click around, learn the interface, maybe ask it to explain itself, and then go back to work.

That is how you learn a normal app. It is not how you learn an agent.

You learn an agent by giving it real work with a low blast radius. Not fake work. Not a toy repo. Not a demo prompt. Real work small enough that you can tell whether it did the job.

Ask it to map a folder you actually use. Ask it to compare two drafts you actually care about. Ask it to clean up a notes file, find stale links on a page, summarize a meeting transcript into decisions and actions, check a local page on mobile, or turn a messy folder into a reading order. Give it something where the answer is not a clever paragraph but a changed artifact, a saved file, a visible diff, a browser screenshot, a terminal result, or a handoff you can reuse.

This matters because Codex trust is not built by being impressed. It is built by checking receipts.

The first useful feeling is not “wow, the model is smart.” The first useful feeling is “I know where to look.” Files shows what changed. Terminal shows what ran. Browser shows what rendered. Review shows the diff. Side chat lets you ask why without derailing the main run. Once you know where the proof lives, the agent stops feeling like a magic box and starts feeling like a junior teammate with a very visible workbench.

That is why the guide starts where it starts. It is not a tour for the sake of a tour. It is a sequence of small runs that teach you how to supervise the work.

And supervision is the point. The goal is not to become passive. It is to become much more precise about what only you should decide.

You decide what done means. You decide what the boundaries are. You decide whether the result is good enough. Codex does the reading, writing, editing, testing, checking, stitching, and retrying that sits between those decisions. The better you get at the loop, the less your week is eaten by the glue work around your actual judgment.

That is the part I wish more people understood. Codex is not just a coding tool. It is a way to move the model into the messy middle of work.


## What you can actually hand it

If you are not a developer, the name is actively unhelpful. Codex sounds like something for people who spend all day in repositories. It is excellent there, obviously. But that is not where the story ends.

The better way to think about Codex is: what work on your computer already has a folder, a source file, a page, a transcript, a spreadsheet, a dashboard, or a repeatable process?

That is the work it can start touching.

For a writer or editor, the first loop might be source-to-draft cleanup: read the transcript, pull the claims, compare against the outline, produce the draft, then leave a list of claims that need checking. Or it might be a voice pass: compare this draft against my best piece, mark where it sounds packaged, rewrite only those sections, and leave a before/after note.

For a founder or operator, the first loop might be weekly signal review: read a folder of notes, compare against last week’s state file, report only what changed, and update the open-loop list. Or it might be customer-support triage: read the latest tickets, group by root cause, flag anything tied to revenue risk, and produce the three replies a human should review.

For a marketer or publisher, the first loop might be page QA: open the landing page, check the mobile layout, click the form, inspect the console, screenshot the broken state, and file the fix. Or it might be launch packaging: take the source draft, produce social variants, check the links, create the image prompts, and leave the publish checklist.

For a team lead, the first loop might be meeting-to-work: turn the transcript into decisions, actions, owners, risks, and a follow-up note, then update the project file. Not a summary. A handoff that makes the next meeting shorter.

For a developer, the loop is obvious: implement the small change, run the tests, review the diff, explain the risk, and prepare the PR. But even there, the real value is not that Codex writes code. It is that Codex can carry a bounded task across the boring middle: find the relevant files, make the edit, run the suite, chase the failure, document what changed, and hand you the review order.

You can do this right now. You do not need to wait for a perfect use case or a company-wide AI strategy. Pick one piece of work that already lives in a file, folder, page, transcript, spreadsheet, dashboard, or repo, and let Codex run a small version of the loop with evidence attached.

That is why I care so much about this guide. The way people fall behind is by waiting for the perfect use case. The way they catch up is by running a small real loop today, then saving what worked so the loop gets faster next time.

### The side panel: five tabs of receipts

The Codex app docks five tabs next to every conversation: Files, Side chat, Review, Terminal, Browser. They exist so you never have to take the agent’s word for anything. The guide’s one-line summary is the one to remember: **the chat talks, the panel proves.** Start here because it teaches the habit that makes the rest of Codex usable: after every run, know exactly where to check what changed.

- **The evidence-pointer habit.** A prompt that makes Codex end every action with a one-line pointer to which tab holds the proof and what you should see there: *Files → src/app.ts shows the new function. Terminal → 14 passed, 0 failed.* And when no panel-visible evidence exists, it has to say “take my word for it” out loud, so even the gaps are visible.
- **Files as your shared map.** After any multi-file change, the fastest answer to “what did you actually touch?” with no git command required. Includes a guided-tour prompt: every file it created or modified with a one-line reason, the five files it would read first if onboarding a new developer, and anything in the tree that surprised it.
- **Side chat: the underrated tab.** A second conversation lane where you ask *why did you choose this approach, what’s the riskiest assumption, what’s plan B*, while the main thread keeps executing, untouched. The rule is simple: if your message is meant to change what Codex does, use the main thread. If it’s meant to change what you understand, use side chat. Questions stop derailing work. I use this constantly.
- **The two-pass review ritual.** Before you read any diff, Codex reviews it as a skeptical senior engineer who didn’t write the code: hunting unintended changes, broken assumptions, missing edges, anything outside the task’s scope, then hands you a prioritized reading order for human eyes. Takes about three minutes. Catches the category of bug that costs an afternoon.
- **Announce-then-run in the Terminal.** One line before every command (what, why, what success looks like), one line after (✓ matched expectation, or ✗ with what differed). Two failures on the same command and it stops and brings it to you instead of trying a third variation silently. Within a week you know the rhythm of normal, which is exactly what makes the abnormal command jump out.
- **The browser toolkit and the un-lying protocol.** The unglamorous controls that earn their keep daily (force reload, device toolbar, clear cache and cookies, screenshot-straight-to-clipboard), plus a step-by-step protocol for the moment the page doesn’t match reality: was it stale state, a build problem, or an actual bug, and what’s the fix.

This is the first catch-up move because people do not get productive with agents by trusting harder. They get productive by knowing what to verify quickly. Once the panel becomes part of the run, Codex is much less intimidating. It can be wrong, but it has to leave tracks.


### Browser & annotations: point instead of describe

Most frustration non-developers have with agents that build things is a translation problem: you can *see* the issue and you’re stuck converting pixels into prose. This page deletes the translation step. Click any element on the page in the Codex browser, type a note in plain language, and Codex receives your words pinned to a screenshot of that exact element. No selectors. No “the third button in the header.”

- **The stack.** Enter sends one note. ⌘Enter stacks a batch. Sweep the whole page like an editor with a red pen, then send the critique as one coordinated work order. This matters more than it looks: five related notes arriving together get implemented as one coherent change, instead of five sequential edits where the third quietly undoes the first. Sweep first, send once, with a prompt that makes Codex check the batch for conflicts before touching code.
- **Queue or steer.** A message sent mid-run either waits politely for the next safe pause or grabs the wheel immediately, and which one your Enter key does is a setting. Not knowing it is how annotations seem to vanish or long tasks seem to wander. The guide makes you learn your default before you trust it during a critical run.
- **The visual bug hunt.** “Seeing X, expected Y, happens when Z,” clicked on the broken thing itself. Codex must name the cause in one line before fixing, and fix the cause, not the symptom.
- **The design review pass.** Sweep spacing, color drift, almost-right type, and send the whole critique as one polish pass. Where several notes point at the same underlying token, it fixes the token, not each instance, and flags any note that fights the project’s established design system instead of silently complying.
- **The copy QA sweep.** Words are UI. Quoted replacement text applies verbatim, while “this is clunky, improve” rewrites in the page’s existing voice. Hard rule: it touches only the copy you clicked. Dramatically better than a copy doc, because every note is attached to its exact context.
- **Explain-this-component.** My favorite play, because annotations aren’t only for changing things. Click a mystery component on an inherited page and ask what it is, where the code lives, and why it’s here. The page becomes a clickable map of its own codebase. Change nothing. It’s a guided tour.

Send this page to anyone who insists agents are an engineers-only tool. Clicking on the thing you mean is not a technical skill. It’s the least technical skill there is.

And it changes the kind of work you can delegate. Visual QA stops being a meeting where someone describes a spacing issue and starts being a marked-up page with instructions attached. Copy cleanup stops being a separate doc and starts happening where the words actually live. Debugging stops with the agent staring at the same broken state you are staring at. That is a small interface feature with a large practical consequence.


### Threading, child threads, and goals: runs, not chat logs

This page gets into the details that make Codex productive: how to scope a thread, when to split work into child threads, how goals keep running, and how to keep a long task from turning into a messy chat log. The basic shift is simple: **a thread is a unit of work, not a chat log.** Scope every thread to one outcome you can state in a single sentence. If you can’t, that’s two threads, because kitchen-sink conversations degrade in quiet ways, with stale context crowding out current intent until the agent serves the conversation’s history instead of your goal.

- **The thread health monitor.** A standing prompt that restates the thread’s goal in one sentence, flags context drift as it happens, and says plainly “this thread should hand off” when a fresh start would serve you better, then writes the handoff itself.
- **Steering and queueing, taught live.** A prompt that has Codex run a multi-step task and tell you, for each message you send mid-run, whether it queued or steered, what your default is, and which of your interjections were worth an interrupt. The rule it leaves you with: queue by reflex, steer on purpose.
- **Parent and child threads.** The pattern that turns Codex from an assistant into a small team: the parent holds the goal, the plan, and the review. Each child gets one step, runs it with clean context, and reports back to one place, workers distinguishable at a glance by their identicons. The guide also shows what failure looks like: if the parent’s transcript is full of file edits, the children aren’t earning their keep.
- **Goal mode, with finish-line craft.** Standard in the app since May: a persistent objective Codex pursues across sessions and your absences. The guide’s interview prompt pins down the verifiable definition of done, the hard boundaries, and the blocked-protocol before anything starts, then has Codex report progress as percent of criteria met, not vibes, and renegotiate rather than satisfy the letter of a broken spec.
- **Handoffs that survive the thread.** Threads end, sprawl, or turn out to live in the wrong project. The handoff summary reads like a nurse’s shift report: here’s the patient, here’s what happened, here’s what’s next, here’s what to watch. A fresh thread with that summary outperforms a long thread with five hundred messages of archaeology. Fresh context beats deep context surprisingly often.

This is where a lot of people either unlock Codex or bounce off it. They treat one thread like one giant relationship with the agent. It becomes the place where everything goes: yesterday’s question, today’s fix, tomorrow’s plan, a half-finished draft, a browser bug, a random aside. Then the thread gets heavy and weird, and the agent starts carrying old assumptions into new work.

The fix is boring and powerful: one thread, one outcome. When the outcome changes, hand off. When the work splits, create child threads. When the job needs to survive your absence, use a goal with a finish line. This is not admin overhead. This is how you make the agent useful for work that takes more than ten minutes.


### The skill library: 31 skills across 7 categories

Skills are how you stop re-explaining your setup. A skill is reusable method: a single-purpose SKILL.md file that states exactly when it should and shouldn’t trigger, living in `~/.codex/skills` for you personally or a repo’s `.agents/skills` to travel with the project. Two universal rules govern every entry: secrets live in env files, never in the skill, and every skill declares its own trigger conditions.

And every entry ends in my favorite trick in the whole guide: a setup prompt you paste into Codex, after which **the agent interviews you for your specifics, writes the skill, installs it where your harness loads from, and tests it.** The skills install themselves.

This is the part that turns one good session into a better next session. If Codex learns how you publish a page, that should not die in the transcript. If it learns how to process your call transcripts, that should not become something you retype next Thursday. If it learns which API endpoint works, where your style guide lives, which files are generated, what QA command catches the recurring bug, or how you want weekly updates formatted, that belongs in a skill or runbook.

That is what people miss when they treat AI as a chat habit. Chat memory is fragile. Skills are durable.

- **Core Infrastructure (5 skills).** Build these first because the other workflows lean on them.

  - An **Image Generation Gateway** so every workflow that makes images shares one fixed-once API connection instead of improvising new mistakes.
  - **Current-Information Search** that routes research through a real-time search API, because agents are often out of date and “confirming” stale training data is the most common failure in AI-assisted research.
  - **Media Transcription** that turns any recording into timestamped, speaker-labeled, chaptered artifacts, the universal input format for everything downstream.
  - **Heavy File Ingestion**, the discipline that converts agent-hostile 200-page PDFs and forty-tab spreadsheets into clean text *before* analysis, because heavy files silently destroy sessions.
  - And an **HTML Artifact Builder** that turns dense output into a single polished, self-contained page in your house style.
- **Research & Thinking (5 skills).**

  - A **Brain Dump Processor** that pans a ten-minute rambling voice memo for gold and hands back the separated ideas with honest evaluations.
  - **Meeting Synthesis** with a fixed format: decisions with who made them, actions with owners, open questions, and a strict line between what was said and what was inferred.
  - A **Weekly Signal Diff** that keeps a state file of what it saw last time and reports only what actually changed, which is the hard part of staying current.
  - An **Assumption Checker** that audits any plan adversarially in a separate pass, listing each assumption, rating how load-bearing and how supported, flagging the single most dangerous one.
  - And a **Reading Pack Builder** that turns a folder of fourteen files into a guided, ordered, progress-tracked review surface.
- **Writing, Voice & Content (4 skills).**

  - A **Personal Voice Skill** built from your real writing across registers, with explicit anti-patterns, so the agent drafts *as* you instead of for you, consistently one of the highest-impact skills for anyone who publishes.
  - A **New Release Briefing** that turns launch-day chaos into your standard package with primary sources and dated claims, so speed stops costing correctness.
  - An **Audience-Calibrated Content System** that encodes a publication’s knowledge floor and ceiling and banned jargon, so every piece starts calibrated.
  - And a **Branded Image Prompting Guide** that writes your visual brand down in prompt-shaped form, so “make me a thumbnail about X” comes back on-brand the first time.
- **Web Publishing & Frontend (4 skills).**

  - A **Frontend Taste System** that replaces the agent’s default instincts (the same hero-and-three-cards page every time) with real typography, restrained color, and mandatory screenshot-inspect-fix verification before anything is called done.
  - A **Personal Site Publisher** that handles everything separating “an HTML file” from “a published page”: clean slug, Open Graph preview image so links unfurl, indexing controls, local verification, deploy.
  - An **Image Model Comparison Arena** that’s the library’s best lesson in composition. It owns only the comparison methodology and delegates generation and publishing to the other skills.
  - And an **Essay Illustration Gallery** that reads a finished piece, picks fifteen to twenty image-worthy moments, locks one style so every frame matches, and ships a captioned gallery.
- **Video & Media Production (3 skills).**

  - These are the clearest demos in the library.
  - **Radio Edit** works from a timestamped transcript to fix the spoken narrative first (best takes chosen, flubs cut) and hands you both a human-reviewable paper edit and a timeline file your editing software imports with the cuts already placed.
  - The **B-Roll Pipeline** shows the operating pattern clearly: a scout agent picks the moments that deserve motion graphics, a builder agent generates them in code against a shared visual contract so fifty graphics look like one designer made them, and the pipeline tracks its own state so a multi-hour job survives interruption.
  - And the **AI Editing Assistant** connects Codex to the editor itself via scripting API (”cut the silences,” “pull every clip where she mentions pricing”) operating on duplicated timelines, never originals.
- **Testing & Quality (3 skills).**

  - The **Testing Runbook Creator** enforces one rule: testing discoveries must not die in chat. Every QA pass leaves a repo-local runbook entry (safe versus destructive actions, seed data, exact verification commands) so session twenty inherits everything sessions one through nineteen learned.
  - **Page Testing Memory** keeps the architecture clean: global skills carry process, repo runbooks carry the selectors and test accounts, and never the reverse.
  - **Browser Automation QA** upgrades “it looks fine” to instruments (Core Web Vitals, console and network capture, device emulation) with screenshots and metrics as evidence.
- **Agent Operations (7 skills).** These are for people who start running Codex often enough that the sessions themselves need management.

  - A **Goal Prompt Generator** that turns a fuzzy plan into a bounded assignment: definition of done, constraints, verification gates, stop conditions.
  - **Visible Delegation**, which runs one agent under another’s supervision in a shared terminal session you can watch and interrupt, because hidden background agents drift for twenty minutes before anyone notices.
  - A **Session Operating Map** so parallel lanes of work stop bottlenecking on your memory of which conversation owned what.
  - **Self-Authored PR Merge** for the daily reality of solo shipping, where review discipline matters more, not less.
  - A **Stakeholder Update Email** with rules I’d tattoo on every agent workflow: only when something real shipped, only what’s true, only in the recipient’s language.
  - The **Session-to-Skill Extractor**, which reviews substantial sessions for patterns worth keeping and drafts them into new skills for your sign-off, the mechanism that makes the library self-multiplying.
  - And the **Agentic Harness Designer**, a design review for anything agent-powered you build, walking permissions, state, evaluation, and observability in order.

The inventory is long because the work is broad. But the mental model is short: every recurring annoyance in your work deserves a loop, and every loop that works twice deserves a skill or runbook.


### The 7 runbooks: skills chained into outcomes

Runbooks are chains of skills that produce a finished outcome. You do not have to remember every step. You ask the agent to execute the chain and make it leave receipts.

- **Talk to Published.** A voice memo recorded on a walk comes out the other end as a drafted, laid-out, deployed page at a clean URL with a proper link preview.
- **Release Day.** Something big ships in your field at 10am. An accurate, on-brand briefing with primary sources is live by noon, and your list knows it. (This one got a serious workout this week.)
- **The Video Production Line.** Raw talking-head footage in, a finished edit with consistent motion graphics out, with the editorial decisions made cheaply on paper before any rendering.
- **Ship a Page You Can Trust.** Build with taste, publish, then verify the live page with instruments rather than vibes, and bank what QA learned so the next deploy verifies in minutes.
- **The Research Engine.** A folder of PDFs, some recordings, and a claim you’re not sure you believe, processed with a chain of custody: every claim traceable to an artifact, every conclusion stress-tested by an adversarial pass that isn’t grading its own homework.
- **Delegate and Verify.** One person running parallel engineering lanes while only touching the two decisions that need a human: what done means, and whether the diff is good.
- **The Flywheel.** The standing cleanup loop under all the others: extract patterns from sessions, bank testing knowledge in repos, keep the global/local boundary clean. No useful discovery dies in chat, which is the mechanism by which everything above compounds.

The runbooks are where Codex starts to feel less like a tool and more like a workbench. You are no longer asking, “Can the model help me with this?” You are asking, “Which chain should run?” That sounds like a small difference until you feel it. A chain has order, proof, a finish line, and a way to get better.

That is also why runbooks are the right format for teams. A team does not adopt “AI”. A team adopts a way of turning a source into an output with checks in the middle: voice memo to article, release to briefing, raw footage to edit, folder to research packet, page to verified publish. That is the level where AI becomes legible to other people.


### The token-burn dashboard: the meter that keeps the whole thing honest

Your AI bill is the most honest report card you have, and Codex is one of the few tools that lets you actually read yours. The app and CLI write local logs with real token usage, measured, not guessed.

The build guide walks you through constructing the dashboard *with* your own agent in a weekend, for free: five views including a log-scale heatmap so one runaway day can’t flatten the quiet ones, burn drivers that turn “I spent a lot” into “I spent a lot on shipping versus research versus video,” and a receipts table where measured and estimated numbers sit side by side, labeled, because estimates never get to cosplay as measurements. I published the standalone version of this guide last week. In the full Codex guide it’s the closing loop. It’s also how “route the cheap work cheap, save the heavy models for whole jobs” stops being a vibe and becomes a rule your tools enforce. The number that matters is cost per shipped outcome. Falling means you’re getting fluent. Rising means you’re just getting expensive.

This matters more as the models get better. When every model was cheap enough to play with casually, waste looked like experimentation. As serious models get more expensive, waste starts looking like bad management. The answer is not to use less AI. The answer is to know what kind of work you are buying.

If a model spent tokens exploring a question that changed your mind, fine. If it spent tokens building an artifact that shipped, fine. If it spent tokens wandering because the task had no finish line, that is a management problem. The dashboard makes that visible. It turns “I used a lot of AI this week” into “I bought research, drafting, QA, video work, and two dead ends.” That is the beginning of judgment.


## The catch-up plan

Don’t read the guide like documentation. Walk it like onboarding. This is the actual weekend. The thing you’re building is a loop, not a study plan: hand Codex a bounded job, inspect the receipts, tighten the instruction, and save the prompt, runbook, or skill that should make the second pass faster than the first.

**Your first day.** Don’t start with a tour. Start with work small enough that you know what “right” looks like. Open a folder you use every week, set Codex to read-only, and ask for a map: what this folder is, which files matter, what is generated, what is stale, and the three safest jobs it could do next. Then pick one reversible job and let it run in ask-before-edits mode: clean up a README, compare two drafts, pull action items from a transcript, fix a broken link, or use a Browser annotation to tighten one piece of page copy. Keep the evidence-pointer prompt on. The first-day goal is to run the loop once: delegate a bounded job, inspect the proof, adjust the instruction, and leave yourself with something you would actually reuse.

The point of day one is not ambition. It is contact. You need to feel the difference between a model answering about work and a model working inside the thing itself. That difference is much easier to feel on a small task than a heroic one. A small task lets you watch the whole loop. You see what Codex read. You see what it changed. You see where it got confused. You see what proof it left. You see what instruction you would improve next time.

That is the catch-up beginning: not mastery, just one real loop.

**Your first week.** Pick a few repeatable loops, not a vague habit. A loop is simple: assign the job, watch the proof, decide what changed, and save the prompt or runbook for next time. Run that pattern across five jobs: map an unfamiliar project, make a tiny edit and show the diff, QA a live or local page with annotations, turn a messy artifact into a clean deliverable, and write a handoff that lets a fresh thread continue the work. Keep every thread to one outcome and learn what your Enter key does mid-run, because queueing versus steering is the difference between supervising a run and constantly interrupting it. By Friday, keep the three loops that earned their place: folder recon, visual QA, and one project-specific runbook or skill. That is when Codex stops being a place you ask questions and becomes a place where next week’s version of the same job starts faster.

Do not measure the first week by how many features you touched. Measure it by how many jobs you would actually run again. A folder-recon loop is useful because every serious project begins with figuring out what is there. A visual-QA loop is useful because every site, deck, dashboard, and page eventually needs someone to look at the actual thing. A handoff loop is useful because every long thread eventually gets stale. None of these is flashy. They’re useful, and that’s what compounds.

**Your first month.** By month one, there are two paths. If yours is “what’s the system underneath?”, read the skill library as architecture: scout/builder decomposition, contract-first generation, resumable state, the global-process-versus-local-facts split. The skills are useful. The patterns are the point, and they travel far beyond my use cases. If yours is “how do I bring this into my organization?”, start with the infrastructure skills, then pick the one runbook that matches your team’s actual work and run it end to end. Runbooks are procedures with receipts attached, not vibes, and that’s exactly the material that survives a skeptical internal review.

By then, you should also have a better instinct for what not to hand over. Codex is excellent at bounded work with sources, artifacts, and verification. It is weaker when you give it a vague wish and no way to know whether the job is done. That is not a knock on Codex. That is the shape of delegation. People are the same. The difference is that Codex will let you improve the assignment and run it again immediately.

**From then on.** If your question is “what call should I make?”, goals plus the meter. Skip the benchmark debates. The brain improves for your competitors the same day it improves for you. Watch whether your people can write a finish line, whether what they learn lands in skills and runbooks or evaporates in chat, and whether cost per shipped outcome is falling. Those numbers move under your management. The leaderboard doesn’t.

This is also where the catch-up starts to feel less frantic. The more loops you have, the less every new model feels like a new curriculum. A better model drops into folder recon, page QA, writing cleanup, video editing, release briefing, support triage, research synthesis, whatever you have built. You still need judgment. You still need taste. You still need to know when the answer is wrong. But you no longer have to invent the work surface every time a model gets better.

That is what feeling caught up actually feels like.
