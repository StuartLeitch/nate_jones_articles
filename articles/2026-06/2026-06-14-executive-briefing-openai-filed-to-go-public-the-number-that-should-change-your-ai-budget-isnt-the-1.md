Last week I got a call from a founder I’ve known for years. He runs an agentic startup, meaning his software does work for customers instead of waiting for customers to work inside the software. He had just moved his product off a frontier model and onto an open-weight model.

His model bill dropped 97 percent in one month.

He kept refreshing the billing dashboard because he did not believe the number.

Two days later, OpenAI filed to go public.

Those are not the same kind of fact. One is a private operating detail from a founder who sees model costs directly in his margins. The other is one of the biggest capital-markets events in technology. But putting them next to each other clarifies the problem leaders now have to think through.

The market story is that intelligence is scarce. OpenAI, Anthropic, and xAI are preparing public investors to value them as if the companies that own the best intelligence will own a huge share of the next decade of enterprise value.

The company story is different. For a growing amount of everyday work, intelligence is getting cheaper very quickly. The hard part is not always getting a smarter model. It is making the company ready to use the intelligence it can already buy.

These IPOs are priced on intelligence. But the work that will matter inside most companies is not just choosing the smartest model. It is deciding how the company needs to change when intelligence becomes cheap enough to put everywhere.

An AI pilot is too small for that question. So is one automated workflow. The harder questions are structural, and they sit in a layer most companies have never had to name.

That layer around the model is the harness.

The model supplies intelligence. The harness supplies the company: the context, documents, permissions, review standards, memory, budgets, decision rights, and accountability that make intelligence useful in a real organization.

The labs can sell you models, tools, even engineers to install them. What they cannot sell you is your own operating context, or the judgment that decides when an output is good enough to ship. Those are the decisions leaders cannot outsource.

A caveat: the S-1s are confidential. Nobody outside the companies, the SEC, and their advisers has read the full documents. The numbers circulating right now are reporting and estimates, and I will treat them that way. What we can read is the public behavior around the filings: the enterprise revenue story, the compute commitments, the pressure from cheaper models, the claims about self-improving AI, and the very human deployment businesses these companies are building around their models.

The question is not whether your company will use these models. It will.

The question is whether the structure around them becomes something you understand and own, or something that happens to you.

**This briefing covers:**

- **The two worlds you now have to operate in.** Public markets are pricing intelligence as scarce, while inside most companies the cost of useful intelligence is collapsing. Both are true, and you have to plan for both.
- **The harness, and why it may be the real scarce asset.** The model supplies intelligence. The company layer around it, meaning context, permissions, review standards, memory, and decision rights, is what turns intelligence into trustworthy work, and it is the part the labs cannot fully sell you.
- **Why the labs are hiring humans to install AI.** The same companies telling investors that machines may soon improve machines are also staffing up to go workflow by workflow inside their customers, which tells you where the hard part actually lives.
- **Five numbers to read when the S-1 opens.** A short scorecard for telling, from the filing itself, whether intelligence stays the scarce asset or whether the value moves to whoever puts it to use.

Wall Street is pricing the bet that intelligence stays scarce. What follows is the part about what your company has to become.

## The IPOs depend on one big idea: intelligence stays valuable

The public-market story starts with scale. Anthropic [filed confidentially for an IPO on June 1](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/06/01/anthropic-ipo-s1-prospectus.html). OpenAI followed on June 8. SpaceX, which absorbed xAI in an all-stock deal in February, [completed the largest IPO on record](https://www.npr.org/2026/06/11/nx-s1-5853199/spacex-ipo-price-elon-musk) on June 12, raising $75 billion at a valuation near $1.8 trillion. [Reporting](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/06/08/openai-confidentially-files-for-ipo-prepping-wall-street-for-ai-debut.html) puts OpenAI’s target valuation as high as $1 trillion. Anthropic’s private marks are in the same neighborhood.

The actual documents are still sealed. That matters. A confidential filing means the public has not yet seen the real revenue mix, margins, customer concentration, contractual commitments, risk factors, or path to profitability. The serious valuation argument has to wait for the real documents.

But the basic story is already clear. These companies need public markets to believe that intelligence remains an extraordinary asset. OpenAI has said enterprise is already more than 40 percent of revenue and is on track to match consumer by the end of this year. Reporting says it has told investors it [projects more than $280 billion in annual revenue by 2030](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-02-20/openai-forecasts-its-revenue-will-top-280-billion-in-2030). It has also told investors it plans to spend around $600 billion on compute through 2030, recalibrated down from a previously promoted $1.4 trillion.

Whatever the final audited numbers say, the shape is obvious: huge infrastructure commitments, enterprise revenue as the growth engine, and valuations that require companies to keep paying for intelligence at scale.

The story has real support. Frontier models are capable. They are opening real work. There are problems where better reasoning matters enormously: scientific research, drug discovery, materials, hard engineering problems, long planning tasks, difficult software work, and any job where being right compounds.

But leaders cannot plan around that story alone.

The whole story has two parts. The top of the market is trying to keep intelligence scarce and expensive. The bottom of the market is pushing useful intelligence toward cheap and abundant. Your company has to operate in both worlds at once.


## A lot of useful intelligence is getting cheaper

The second story starts with the bill. His product still needed AI. It still needed agents to understand requests, use tools, and produce outputs customers could trust. But the cost of running that intelligence collapsed when he moved to an open-weight model.

That does not mean frontier models are doomed. It does not mean every job should move to the cheapest model tomorrow. It means something narrower and more useful: a growing amount of routine AI work is becoming cheap enough that the model itself stops being the hardest part of the business problem.

Flo Crivello at Lindy has been making a similar point in public. Lindy runs agents continuously for customers, so inference cost is not theoretical. In April, Crivello wrote that inference had become Lindy’s biggest cost, bigger than payroll, and that open models had gone from “not even close” to frontier for many use cases in roughly a year. In June, he said Lindy [moved its traffic off Anthropic’s models and onto DeepSeek](https://thenewstack.io/lindy-deepseek-anthropic-switch/), saving millions of dollars a year while improving performance on many core use cases.

That pressure is showing up everywhere. GitHub had to move Copilot toward usage-based pricing after agentic coding sessions consumed more than flat-rate pricing could support. Uber reportedly [burned through its entire 2026 AI budget in four months](https://natesnewsletter.substack.com/p/ai-token-cost-management), much of it on agentic coding. The Linux Foundation now has a Tokenomics Foundation because enterprises need ways to measure and manage token costs.

That creates a budget problem. Companies cannot let every task run on the most expensive model forever. They need [routing](https://natesnewsletter.substack.com/p/opus-48-benchmark-model-selection), measurement, and a clear read on which jobs actually improve when they spend more on intelligence.

But budget is still not the deepest issue.

The deeper issue is that cheaper intelligence does not automatically make the company better. A cheaper model can summarize faster. It can classify more cheaply. It can draft for almost nothing. But if the company does not know where the right documents are, what standard the output has to meet, who reviews it, and who owns the result, the work still gets stuck.

If intelligence gets cheaper and your company still cannot use it well, the bottleneck was not the model.


## The hard part is making AI work inside the company

The hard part is not buying intelligence. The hard part is making your company usable by intelligence.

It sounds strange until you try to automate anything important.

The AI needs the brief, but the brief is not written down. It needs the source of truth, but the source of truth is split across four systems. It needs examples of good work, but the examples live in old emails and a few people’s memory. It needs to know who can approve the output, but the formal owner is not the person who actually decides. It needs to know what risk is acceptable, but the answer changes by customer, contract, and quarter.

Many AI projects look impressive in demos and then slow down inside real companies. The model can do the task in isolation. The company has not made the task clear enough, connected enough, and reviewable enough to run reliably.

That is not a model problem. It is not even only a software problem. It is an organization problem.

The company has to decide what the AI is allowed to know, what it is allowed to touch, what it is allowed to spend, when a human checks the work, what proof is required, and who is accountable after the output leaves the sandbox. Those decisions are not decoration around the model. They are the difference between a useful system and an expensive demo.


## The harness is the company around the model

A harness is everything around the model that lets the model do useful work.

It includes the obvious technical pieces: tools, connectors, permissions, memory, logs, evals, and monitoring.

But the important pieces are not only technical. The harness also includes the job definition, the examples of good work, the review standard, the escalation path, the budget, the human owner, and the decision about what happens when the AI changes the way the work used to move.

A prompt is not a harness.

A spec is not a harness.

A pilot is not a harness.

Those can be parts of a harness, but the harness is bigger. It is the system that lets machine intelligence enter the company and produce work that the company can actually use.

Individual users already have versions of this. [Claude Code and Codex are harnesses](https://natesnewsletter.substack.com/p/claude-code-vs-codex-agents) in a practical sense. They give the model a workspace, task state, tools, permission boundaries, review surfaces, and a way to return proof. The same underlying intelligence can feel different in different products because the surrounding system changes the work.

At the company level, the harness is much harder.

It is not just a better interface. It is the company deciding how machine work should run through the organization. Where does the AI get context? Which work can it do on its own? Which work requires review? Which review can be sampled? Which review requires sign-off? What should be measured? What should be remembered? What has to be portable if the company switches models?

These are operational questions with structural consequences. The answers change how work moves, what managers own, and what parts of the company become reusable.

If AI can do more of the work, managers cannot only manage people doing tasks. They have to manage systems that produce work. They have to know what context the system uses, how outputs are checked, where exceptions go, and when the system is making the organization better or simply making bad work faster.

That makes the harness bigger than AI adoption.


## The labs know this, because they are hiring people to install AI

If the models were enough, enterprise deployment would look mostly self-serve. The labs are doing something else.

OpenAI launched the [OpenAI Deployment Company](https://openai.com/index/openai-launches-the-deployment-company/) with more than $4 billion in initial capital, embedding engineers inside organizations working on complex problems. The launch included acquiring Tomoro, an applied-AI engineering firm, to bring roughly 150 forward-deployed engineers in from day one. OpenAI Frontier is framed as a serious enterprise platform with shared business context, identity and permissions, and evaluation. It also comes with embedded engineers attached.

Anthropic announced an [enterprise services firm with Blackstone, Hellman & Friedman, and Goldman Sachs](https://www.anthropic.com/news/enterprise-ai-services-company) to help companies rebuild workflows around Claude. The announcement said enterprise demand for Claude is outpacing any single delivery model.

Google Cloud is ramping up forward-deployed engineer hiring too, with CEO Thomas Kurian citing rising client demand. Box CEO Aaron Levie has said forward-deployed engineers are becoming one of the most important roles in AI rollouts. Postings for the role were [up roughly 729 percent over the past year](https://www.aol.com/articles/job-postings-tech-role-grown-185134480.html), by one industry measure.

The labs are not only selling intelligence. They are building services businesses because [companies need help making intelligence useful](https://natesnewsletter.substack.com/p/enterprise-ai-deployment-layer).

The same companies telling investors that machines may soon improve machines are also hiring humans to go company by company, workflow by workflow, and wire the systems into place. That does not mean the models are weak. It means the business environment around the models is messy.

The labs can bring engineers. They can bring tools. They can bring frontier models. They can bring deployment patterns. They still have to enter your company and learn how it works.


## Smarter models do not fix unclear companies

The recursive self-improvement claims are important, but they can make leaders draw the wrong conclusion.

Anthropic has argued that AI systems may be approaching the point where they help design and build their successors. The evidence from inside the labs is serious: Claude now writes [more than 80 percent of the code](https://www.anthropic.com/institute/recursive-self-improvement) merged into Anthropic’s own codebase, up from low single digits before Claude Code launched, alongside gains on difficult internal tasks and parts of training work. OpenAI has also discussed recursive self-improvement as an explicit research concern.

Take that seriously. In some domains, more intelligence may change the outcome dramatically.

But look at where the best evidence comes from. A frontier lab is probably one of the most AI-ready organizations in the world. It has codebases, tests, logs, evals, permission systems, review norms, and engineers who understand how to inspect machine output. The lab is already organized around the work of improving models.

Of course smarter models show up there first.

Most companies are not like that.

Most companies have scattered documents, informal decision rights, unclear definitions of done, messy systems of record, and important context living in people’s heads. A smarter model can reason better inside that environment, but it cannot magically make the environment clear.

It cannot know which document is the real policy if the company itself has three conflicting versions.

It cannot know which approval matters if the org chart says one thing and the real decision process says another.

It cannot know what “good” means if “good” is a manager’s taste that has never been made explicit.

It cannot decide whether a workflow should exist if the company has never asked whether the old process still makes sense.

The mistake in a lot of AI strategy is assuming that smarter models automatically shrink the company’s adoption problem. Some of it will shrink. A better model will make some tasks easier. But the harder the work gets, the more the model needs a clear company around it.

Smarter models raise the value of the harness. They do not remove the need for one.


## The real question is how the company should change

If intelligence becomes cheap enough to put everywhere, then the company has to ask what still needs to be organized the old way.

Some teams are built around people passing work from one step to the next. Some manager roles are built around assigning tasks, chasing status, and checking outputs that may soon be produced by systems. Some approval processes exist because the company lacked a better way to control risk. Some reporting layers exist because information used to be hard to gather, format, and interpret.

AI does not automatically eliminate any of that. But it does force the question.

Which parts of the company are doing judgment?

Which parts are moving information?

Which parts are checking work?

Which parts are there because the company never built a better system?

Those questions are uncomfortable because they ask whether the organization is still shaped around an old constraint.

Electricity helps here only if we take the analogy seriously. Factories did not transform because someone plugged in a motor. They transformed when leaders redesigned the factory around electric power. The old layout had been shaped by steam, shafts, belts, and the need to route mechanical force through a building. Once power could be routed differently, the factory could be organized differently.

That did not happen automatically. It took management work.

AI raises a similar question. Not because AI is exactly electricity, but because a new input is becoming widely available and the value will not go evenly to everyone who buys it. It will go to the organizations that change around it.


## What leaders actually have to own

Leaders have to decide what the company itself should own.

The labs can sell a lot. They can sell models, agents, enterprise platforms, deployment engineers, eval tools, memory products, connectors, and consulting. Many of those things will be useful. Some will become standard infrastructure.

But leaders should be careful about treating the whole harness as something to buy.

Some parts of the harness are just plumbing. Buy them if they work. You do not need to build every connector or eval dashboard from scratch.

Other parts are company property in a deeper sense.

Company memory is not just a knowledge base. It is the durable context that explains how the company thinks, what it has decided, what good work looks like, what customers expect, what risks it avoids, and what tradeoffs leadership has already made.

Review standards go beyond QA. They are how the company decides whether machine work is safe, useful, complete, and aligned with the business.

Permissions are not just IT settings. They are decisions about what work an agent is allowed to do, which systems it can touch, and where the company draws the line between assistance and action.

Definitions of done are more than process notes. They are the way judgment becomes reusable.

If those things accumulate entirely inside a vendor system, the vendor is not only providing AI. It is becoming part of the company’s operating memory. That may be acceptable in some cases. But it should be a conscious decision, not an accident.

The opposite failure is just as real. If every team builds its own harness, the company ends up with scattered agent workflows, inconsistent review, no shared memory, unclear model routing, and no way to know what is actually happening. That looks entrepreneurial for six months and then becomes a mess.

The decision is not “centralize everything” or “let teams move fast.” It is more precise:

Which parts of the harness should be shared company infrastructure?

Which parts should live close to the team because they depend on domain judgment?

Which parts can be bought?

Which parts must remain portable?

Which parts encode the company too deeply to outsource casually?


## The budget has to follow the work

AI budgets have to change too.

The first AI budget question was simple: how much are we spending on tools and model access?

That question is now too small.

Companies need to know which jobs deserve expensive intelligence and which jobs should run on cheaper models. They need to [measure token usage against outcomes](https://natesnewsletter.substack.com/p/token-burn-dashboard), not just usage against budget. I have written about this in the Token Burn work: the point is not to brag about how many tokens you used or panic because the number is high. The point is to know what work the tokens moved and whether the result was worth repeating.

But the budget has to go beyond model spend.

If the harness is where the value moves, then companies have to budget for the harness. That means context maintenance, review systems, internal evals, model-routing infrastructure, permission design, workflow redesign, and people who understand enough about both the business and the tools to connect them.

The FDE boom matters because the labs are pricing in the fact that deployment takes people. The question is whether all of those people work for the vendor, or whether some of that capability develops inside the company.

For leaders, the budget question becomes:

What are we paying the model to do?

What are we paying people to review?

What context are we maintaining so AI can work here?

What systems are we building so this does not become fifty disconnected experiments?

What organizational changes are we avoiding by calling the problem technical?


## Five numbers I will read before the valuation

When the sealed document finally opens, most of the coverage will run at the headline number. I understand the pull. But the valuation is not the most useful thing in the filing. The most useful thing is a short list of numbers that tell you which of the two worlds you are operating in. They are not only numbers for investors, and you do not need the document to start watching for them in your own vendors.

The first is gross margin against compute. This is the software-or-utility question in one line. OpenAI’s adjusted gross margin [already slipped to around 33 percent in 2025, down from about 40 percent the year before](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/openai-sees-compute-spend-around-223950561.html), as inference costs climbed. If margin holds while usage grows, intelligence is behaving like software, and the platform keeps most of the value. If margin keeps compressing as usage grows, it is behaving more like a metered utility, and the value starts moving to whoever builds on top.

Second, watch the quality of enterprise revenue, not the size of it. A big enterprise number tells you almost nothing on its own. Revenue welded into workflows that would hurt to leave is an asset. Revenue renting a model until a cheaper one clears the bar is a flow that can walk out the door in a quarter. Retention, customer concentration, and the share sitting under long contracts are where you read which kind you are looking at.

The third number is capital commitments. The material-contracts section is where the compute bill stops being a press-release figure and turns into money the company is legally on the hook for, signed and fixed and in many cases owed whether or not the demand arrives. Read it for the binding constraint: whether the next few years are gated by software, by talent, or by physical capacity. Large commitments are not automatically a problem. They do convert a soft growth story into a hard schedule of payments that has to be met on time.

Services dependence is the fourth. I want to see how much of the growth rides on deployment headcount, partners, and custom engagements. Real software scales without adding people. If this revenue only climbs when the company adds bodies and projects, the multiple it has earned looks closer to a consultancy than a software platform, however the story gets told on the roadshow.

Last is pricing power, which is the question the whole filing keeps circling. Across the periods it discloses, are prices firm because customers cannot get the same result for less, or are they sliding as open models close the distance? Firm pricing means the lab keeps most of what it gains from getting smarter. Sliding pricing means those gains leak out to the rest of us building on top. If that is what the numbers show, it is the best news in the filing for almost everyone who is not a shareholder.

I want to be careful not to overstate what five numbers can do. They will not settle whether AI matters. They tell you something narrower and more useful: whether intelligence is the scarce asset, or whether the scarce asset is the work of putting it to use. That is the question your own company is already answering, one vendor decision at a time.


## Where this could be wrong

There are two ways this argument could be wrong.

The first is that frontier models pull away. If recursive self-improvement creates a durable lead and open models stop catching up on routine work, then intelligence remains scarce for longer than I expect. In that world, the labs capture more value, frontier spend deserves a larger share of the budget, and the utility analogy gets weaker.

The second is that deployment gets much easier. If the labs turn company installation into software faster than the current services push suggests, then the harness layer becomes more productized. Companies would still need to make decisions about context, review, ownership, and structure, but the work would be easier and faster than I am describing.

Both are possible.

Neither lets leaders avoid the core question.

Even if frontier models pull away, the company still has to know where more intelligence actually changes the outcome. Even if deployment gets easier, the company still has to decide who owns its memory, permissions, review standards, and definitions of done.

Better software can reduce the burden. It cannot make the company understand itself.


## The question to take seriously

The IPO coverage will spend the summer asking whether OpenAI, Anthropic, and xAI are worth the valuations being discussed. Investors have to answer that.

For companies, the question is different: if intelligence becomes much more powerful and much cheaper at the same time, what does your organization have to become?

Not “which model should we use?”

Not “what pilot should we run?”

Not “how do we encourage adoption?”

Those questions matter, but they are downstream.

The upstream question is whether the company is ready to make its own work legible to machines. That means memory, context, access, review, ownership, decision rights, and structure. It means asking which parts of the organization are doing judgment and which parts are moving work through an old process because that was the only option before.

The AI labs are going public on the story that intelligence is the scarce asset. Their own deployment work suggests the harder asset may be the company layer around intelligence.

That layer is the harness.

Not a prompt. Not a pilot. Not a workflow trick. The system that decides what AI knows, what it can touch, who checks it, who owns it, and how the organization changes when machine work becomes normal.

If you let a vendor own that layer by default, the vendor becomes more than a model provider.

If you let every team invent it alone, the company becomes harder to manage.

If you understand it and take responsibility for it, AI becomes less like a magic feature and more like a new input the company can actually reorganize around.
