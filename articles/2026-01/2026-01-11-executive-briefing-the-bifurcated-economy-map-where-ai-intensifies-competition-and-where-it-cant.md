## **The Bifurcated Economy: How AI Is Reshaping Competition Unevenly**

Most executives are working from a flawed map. The story they’ve absorbed says AI-native startups will disrupt slow-moving incumbents, giants need to accelerate transformation or die, and the firms that adopt AI fastest will win. This narrative feels urgent and actionable. It’s also misleading in specific, consequential ways.

Consider what just happened in enterprise IT services. Many observers expected AI to finally disintermediate the traditional services layer. The logic seemed straightforward: Cognition builds Devin, an AI coding agent that handles engineering work. Clients buy directly from Cognition. Infosys and other traditional IT services firms get squeezed out of the middle. That’s the “AI disrupts everything” narrative applied to a $200 billion industry.

But Cognition didn’t go hunting for enterprise clients. They partnered with Infosys, who will deploy Devin across their engineering organization and global client base. The AI startup is selling *to* the incumbent, not competing with them. And this pattern is repeating across multiple Valley unicorns. As one observer in San Francisco noted: “Somehow, the very inefficiency and anti-innovation inertia of traditional IT services is what keeps them relevant. They don’t move fast, but they still own solid relationships with enterprises.”

The expected disruption is happening. But the arrow points the opposite direction from what most observers predicted. The giants aren’t falling to AI-native startups. They’re absorbing them.

**If you’re a giant** and you’re panicking about startup disruption, you may be solving the wrong problem. Your distribution moat is more durable than you think—but your internal talent retention and innovation velocity are more urgent than you realize.

**If you’re mid-tier** and you’re investing in AI to make your current model more efficient, you’re probably just dying slower. The squeeze from above and below is real, and incremental efficiency won’t escape it.

**If you’re in physical or local services** and vendors are selling you “AI transformation,” you may be buying something you don’t need. Your market isn’t becoming more contestable. Basic back-office automation is the play, not strategic reinvention.

**If you’re building or funding AI startups** on the thesis that capability alone wins, the Cognition playbook should give you pause. Distribution ate capability. The startup with the better tech sold to the incumbent with the better relationships.

The framework in this article isn’t “AI changes everything” or “AI changes nothing.” It’s a specific map of where competition intensifies, where it doesn’t, and what that means for capital allocation and strategic positioning. The core insight is that AI is not uniformly reshaping competition—it’s bifurcating the economy in ways that create clear winners and losers depending on where you sit.

## **The Standard Story and Its Limits**

The conventional wisdom runs something like this: AI-native startups move fast, adopt new tools constantly, and build from day one without legacy constraints. Big companies are slow, bureaucratic, burdened by legacy systems and legacy thinking. The disruptors will win; the incumbents will fall. We’ve seen this movie before.

This story isn’t wrong about everything, but it misses crucial dynamics. The giants are not nearly as vulnerable as the narrative suggests. Many of them have moats that AI cannot erode. And the startups may not be quite as threatening as advertised, because the capabilities they’re selling are often getting cheaper by the month—which is a problem if your whole value proposition is the capability rather than the distribution.

The Cognition-Infosys partnership illustrates why the bottleneck isn’t production capability. It’s distribution. Cognition has first-layer capability in abundance—they can generate code, handle migrations, produce engineering output. What they don’t have is embedded relationships with hundreds of enterprises, existing procurement contracts, trust built over decades of delivery, or the liability wrappers and accountability structures that enterprise buyers require before deploying anything into production systems. Infosys has all of that.

So you get a trade that makes perfect sense once you have the right model: Cognition gets instant distribution without burning capital on enterprise sales cycles. Infosys gets capability differentiation they couldn’t build internally at the same pace. The enterprise client gets AI-powered delivery from a vendor they already trust.

This isn’t an isolated case. The pattern is repeating across the professional services economy:

**Harvey**, the legal AI startup, didn’t try to disintermediate law firms or the Big Four. Instead, it partnered with PwC—an exclusive alliance among the Big Four—to deploy its AI across 4,000+ legal professionals in 100+ countries. Harvey is now valued at roughly $8 billion, and PwC’s junior lawyers reportedly say they’d “riot” if the tool were taken away. The AI startup sells *through* the incumbent’s distribution, not around it.

**Salesforce** has been acquiring AI startups at pace: Doti AI for approximately $100 million, Convergence.ai for its agent technology, Qualified for $1-1.5 billion. Each acquisition follows the same logic—Salesforce has the distribution (76 of the Top 2000 North American online retailers use its platform), and the startups have capability that Salesforce couldn’t build as fast internally.

**The Big Five consulting firms** are all making the same play. Accenture is acquiring Faculty, a UK-based AI-native services company. McKinsey acquired Iguazio for its AI platform capabilities. Bain acquired Max Kelsen. Deloitte acquired OpTeamizer for AI on NVIDIA GPUs. Accenture has committed $3 billion to expanding its AI practice and plans to double its AI workforce to 80,000 specialists.

The pattern is consistent: AI capability is flowing toward distribution, not displacing it. The startups with the best technology are selling to the incumbents with the best relationships.

Meanwhile, the place where firms are actually in trouble is in between—a dynamic the conventional wisdom misses by focusing only on startups versus giants. The middle tier of the professional services and software industries is getting crushed. Think the marketing agency with forty employees, the IT consultancy that’s been around for fifteen years, the software development shop that serves mid-market clients, the design firm that built a reputation on being reliable and professional over the past couple of decades. Those firms are getting squeezed from both directions: from below by tiny teams using AI to produce work nearly indistinguishable from what a mid-tier firm can deliver, and from above by giants with distribution advantages they cannot match. They have no obvious escape route.


## **The Starting Point Most People Miss**

If you treat AI as “automation,” you end up arguing about labor displacement and headcount. If you treat AI as a general-purpose technology that collapses the marginal cost of cognition, you end up in a different place: AI changes what is scarce.

That sounds abstract until you line it up with two economic concepts that have been around for decades but suddenly matter a great deal.

The first is Jevons paradox. When a capability gets cheaper, we tend to use more of it, not less. William Stanley Jevons noticed in the 1860s that more efficient steam engines didn’t reduce coal consumption—they exploded the usage of steam power by making it economical for new applications. Efficiency removed a cost barrier, and demand rushed in.

The second is Baumol’s cost disease. As some sectors of the economy get more productive, wages tend to rise economy-wide, which means sectors that *don’t* get more productive become relatively more expensive. The classic example is orchestras: it still takes the same number of musicians to play a Mozart quartet as it did in 1790, but musicians today get paid enormously more because they could otherwise go work in industries where productivity has increased. Services that resist productivity improvement don’t disappear—they get more expensive.

AI can produce both effects at once, even within the same job, as “the last 1% that must be human” becomes the throughput bottleneck.

That’s the macro frame. But the micro frame—the part that makes this about firms living and dying—is that firms already differ wildly in productivity, even within the same narrowly defined industry, and those differences persist. Census Bureau data on productivity dispersion shows that within industries, the gap between high and low performers is often multiples, not percentages.

So the real question isn’t “does AI raise productivity.” It’s: when you introduce near-free cognition into an economy where firm performance already varies massively, do markets finally become brutal enough to kill the long tail? Or does the long tail survive because the reasons it exists aren’t primarily about cognition?

My thesis is that both happen, but not as a mushy compromise—as a structured bifurcation. AI increases competitive selection pressure dramatically in markets where output becomes more substitutable and contestable, which does kill long tails in those domains. At the same time, AI increases the viability of small firms in many local, relationship-heavy, and capacity-constrained markets by lowering fixed “coordination overhead,” so the long tail persists there—and often gets more expensive (Baumol) rather than disappearing.

The shape of 2025 and 2026 is not “everything concentrates” or “nothing changes.” It’s a barbell: hyper-competition and consolidation in some markets; persistent fragmentation with inflation in others; and a lot of mid-tier firms getting crushed in the middle.


## **Jevons and Baumol Aren’t Just Macro Stories—They’re a Map of the Firm**

Once you accept the Jevons-Baumol frame, you can stop talking about “AI replacing workers” and start talking about where the bottleneck moves.

Inside a firm, you can think of work as three layers.

The first layer is tokenizable cognition—drafting, analysis, planning, routine coding, summarization, templated communications. This is where AI makes marginal cost collapse. Jevons dominates here: the firm doesn’t do less; it does far more. More variants, more segmentation, more internal reporting, more experiments, more first drafts to choose from.

The second layer is accountability and exception-handling—sign-off, risk ownership, escalation calls, “who is liable,” “who gets fired,” persuasion, cross-functional negotiation. This is where AI often *increases* scarcity. The more throughput you generate in the first layer, the more you need trusted gatekeeping in the second.

The third layer is embodied, local execution—installation, repair, caregiving, on-site service, physical capacity, real-world interactions. This is classic Baumol territory: it doesn’t scale like software, and rising economy-wide wages raise its price.

The weirdness of 2025 is that AI floods the first layer with abundance, and that flood forces the firm to confront the second and third layers as the real constraints.

If your firm mostly sells first-layer work—the drafting, the analysis, the code—you have a problem. The work is becoming commoditized. Your competitors can produce it at similar quality, often at lower cost. What you’re selling is no longer special or scarce.

If your firm’s value was always in the second layer—in relationships, accountability, and good taste—you’re in better shape. You still need to produce good first-layer work, but now you can produce it cheaper while continuing to compete on judgment.

And if you’re in the third layer, the fundamental economics currently favor you.

The Cognition-Infosys partnership maps cleanly onto this framework. Cognition dominates first-layer production; Infosys owns second-layer infrastructure—the relationships, the accountability structures, the enterprise trust. Neither could easily build what the other has. So they trade.


## **Why Productivity Dispersion Matters**

If firms were already close to each other in efficiency, then a general-purpose productivity tool might lift everyone and not change competitive structure much. But we’re not in that world.

Census Bureau data on productivity dispersion reveals that within-industry variation is large, persistent, and economically meaningful. If you look at four-digit manufacturing industries, the median difference between 75th and 25th percentile firms is roughly 1.7x on total factor productivity. Between 90th and 10th percentile, it’s roughly 3x. And in some industries like semiconductors, the 90-10 dispersion exceeds 30x.

Two implications follow. First, within an industry, a “typical” firm is not competing against peers who are a little better. It’s competing against peers who can be multiples better. Second, even in something as standardized as manufacturing output, dispersion is large and doesn’t automatically collapse.

This is why I don’t buy simple “AI kills all inefficient producers” narratives. Competitive markets have tolerated massive dispersion for decades. The reasons aren’t mysterious if you focus on what firms actually sell: not just “units of output,” but bundles that include trust, geography, relationships, timing, integration, and brand.

And there’s a second body of work that reinforces this: persistent productivity differences have been tied to persistent differences in management quality and operating practices. The research of Nicholas Bloom and John Van Reenen is basically a long argument that management practices vary widely and correlate strongly with productivity, helping explain persistent performance gaps.

So the micro picture before AI already looked like this: some firms are run dramatically better than others, and the world doesn’t cleanse that quickly. AI lands on this backdrop. It doesn’t produce instant convergence. It produces a widening gap between firms that can re-architect workflows and those that cannot.


## **The Firm-Death Mechanism: AI Increases Selection Pressure Where It Increases Contestability**

“AI accelerates firm velocity” is real. But velocity only turns into deaths when it changes one of the underlying determinants of competition: contestability.

A market becomes more contestable when buyers can evaluate quality cheaply, switching costs fall, output is more substitutable, geography matters less, and entrants can reach customers without massive fixed costs.

AI pushes on all of these in many digital contexts. It lowers the fixed cost of producing marketing, content, sales outreach, product copy, and support scripts. It lowers the labor needed for back-office functions. It makes prototyping faster, which increases iteration speed. It increasingly standardizes what “good enough” looks like in many cognitive tasks.

So yes: in any market where the main differentiator was “we can execute this kind of cognitive work faster or cheaper or better,” AI makes competition sharper.

But AI does not push equally on all markets. In some markets, it increases velocity but does not increase contestability much, because the bottlenecks are not cognitive production.

So the firm-death thesis becomes: AI kills long tails in markets where it collapses differentiation faster than it creates new moats. It doesn’t kill long tails where the moat is non-cognitive scarcity—trust, locality, regulation, embodied service, or deep switching costs.

That’s not a “maybe.” It’s a direct consequence of the bottleneck-shift model.


## **Where AI Kills Long Tails (and Why)**

There is a set of markets where the “fiercer competition leads to more deaths” view will look obviously correct in retrospect. These are markets where the output is digital, quality is legible (buyers can tell what’s good quickly), switching is easy, and a distribution layer can aggregate demand.

In those markets, AI changes the competitive game in three ways at once.

**First, it commoditizes the baseline.** When everyone can produce competent copy, competent analysis, competent code scaffolds, competent decks, competent outbound messages, the value of “competent production” collapses. That doesn’t mean all providers become equal—it means the minimum bar rises and the “mediocre but acceptable” segment loses its price umbrella. This is one of the cleanest ways to kill the long tail: not by making the best firm a little better, but by making the bottom 60% unable to charge for what used to be their core competence.

**Second, it increases iteration speed, which compounds.** If iteration speed doubles, you don’t just ship twice as fast—you learn twice as fast. You run twice as many experiments, converge on product-market fit faster, tune funnels faster, tune pricing faster. In markets where speed of learning matters, iteration is a compounding advantage. This is where long tails die: because “good enough” and “slow” stops being viable at the same time.

**Third, it centralizes around distribution.** When outputs commoditize, differentiation tends to move to distribution: brand, embeddedness, default channels, platforms, procurement relationships, bundling. In many digital categories, that creates winner-take-most dynamics.

In these domains, I’m not just open to “AI kills the long tail.” I think it’s a strong base case. The reason I still don’t claim “AI kills the long tail everywhere” is because this logic is contingent on substitutability. And a lot of the economy is structurally not substitutable in that way.


## **Where AI Won’t Kill Long Tails—and May Actually Strengthen Them**

There’s another big set of markets where AI is not a long-tail killer. It’s a fixed-cost reducer.

These markets are typically local, relationship-heavy, regulated or liability-gated, or capacity-constrained by embodied labor.

Here AI doesn’t make the service globally substitutable. Instead, it lowers overhead: scheduling, invoicing, marketing, customer communications, quoting, compliance paperwork, basic bookkeeping. That means more small firms can survive with fewer staff and less fragility.

This is the part that looks counterintuitive until you combine it with Baumol. As the AI-rich parts of the economy get more productive, wages rise, and embodied local services become more expensive (Baumol dynamics). We get richer as a society and therefore pay more for things that didn’t become more productive.

So the world that emerges isn’t “the small service provider dies.” It can be “the small service provider charges more and uses AI to stay lean.”

In other words: in these markets, AI increases the viability of the long tail, and Baumol increases the pricing power of the long tail.

Consider a plumbing company. The firm sells third-layer work: a human being shows up at your house and fixes the problem. AI helps this firm by automating scheduling, dispatch, and invoicing. But AI doesn’t threaten the firm the way it threatens a marketing agency, because the plumbing market isn’t becoming more contestable. No matter how good AI gets, you cannot switch to a plumber in Hong Kong tomorrow. The plumber competes locally, in person, at a specific time and place.

That’s a very different equilibrium than the digital commodity case. And it explains the barbell: hyper-competition in contestable digital markets, persistent fragmentation with rising prices in embodied local markets.


## **The Most Important 2025 Wrinkle: The Adoption J-Curve**

Even if you buy the contestability split, there’s another firm-death mechanism that is under-discussed: transition fragility.

Evidence from manufacturing suggests AI adoption follows a J-curve pattern where short-term performance losses precede longer-term gains. Those losses are unevenly distributed, concentrating among older businesses, while earlier adopters show stronger growth over time conditional on survival.

Read that slowly: it’s not “AI gives everyone productivity.” It’s “AI can harm you before it helps you, and survival is conditional.”

The pattern is already visible. Klarna replaced 700 customer service employees with AI, but quality declined, customers revolted, and the company had to rehire humans. Amazon’s “Just Walk Out” retail technology, marketed as AI-powered, turned out to rely heavily on remote workers in India monitoring cameras. These aren’t failures of AI capability—they’re failures to traverse the adoption valley, where the disruption costs hit before the productivity gains materialize.

That gives you a firm-death thesis that doesn’t rely on perfect contestability. Some firms will die not because they were outcompeted in steady state, but because they cannot traverse the adoption valley—they incur disruption costs (process changes, work-in-progress effects, labor reconfiguration, loss of structured management practices) and they don’t have the managerial capacity or financial slack to reach the upside.

This is exactly how past general-purpose technologies played out: gains are real, but they require complementary investments and reorganization. The path is not smooth.

In 2025 terms, this is how you get simultaneous headlines of “AI adoption is everywhere” and “productivity isn’t showing up broadly yet.” You see wide adoption but narrow realized value.


## **The Labor-Market Evidence Supports Complements and Bottlenecks, Not Wipeout**

The Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond study on generative AI in customer support is useful because it shows something precise: average productivity up roughly 14%, but gains concentrated among novice and low-skill workers, with minimal impact on highly experienced workers.

That pattern matters for firm dynamics in two ways. First, AI can compress within-firm skill dispersion by making low-skill workers more effective faster. That can raise the performance floor. Second, the fact that gains are heterogeneous reinforces the bigger point: benefits depend on context and workflow design. Real-world deployment interacts with training, management, and process.

This is more evidence that the economy doesn’t instantly converge. The “AI wipes out workers” story and the “AI changes nothing” story are both wrong. The real story is complements and bottlenecks: AI amplifies some workers, creates new constraints around others, and the net effect depends heavily on how firms reorganize.


## **The Macro Adoption Reality Suggests We’re in Diffusion, Not Cleansing**

St. Louis Fed tracking of generative AI adoption provides a sanity check. By August 2025, overall adoption among adults 18-64 was around 54.6%. Work use was rising more modestly—from roughly 33.3% to 37.4% over twelve months. And the share of work hours spent using generative AI rose from about 4.1% in November 2024 to roughly 5.7% by August 2025.

This matters because if only 5-6% of work hours are being directly mediated by generative AI tools by mid-to-late 2025, we are not yet in a world where “AI has fully rewritten competitive selection.” We’re in a world where adoption is broad but shallow, value capture is uneven, and reorganization costs are dominating.

Which is exactly the setup where the J-curve firm-death mechanism bites: firms that can reorganize win later; firms that can’t start bleeding early.


## **The Capital-Market Lever: Whether Firms Actually Die Depends on the Exit Channel**

Here’s the brutal truth about “more competition should mean more deaths”: it only means that if failure is allowed and financed failure is not perpetually postponed.

Research on zombie firms—businesses that are economically unviable but survive due to continued financing—demonstrates that when financial pressure is low and restructuring is delayed, low-productivity firms can persist and “congest” markets. They tie up resources and crowd out more productive firms. Bank of International Settlements research is clear about the mechanism: zombie prevalence can rise over time, and zombies are less productive and can crowd out investment and employment at healthier firms.

So in 2025, you have a three-way interaction. AI increases selection pressure in some markets. AI lowers operating costs for many firms, which can actually keep weak firms alive longer. And interest rates, credit conditions, and restructuring regimes determine whether weak firms actually exit.

This is why AI alone does not determine cleansing. The financial system determines whether the cleansing happens quickly, slowly, or not at all.


## **The Middle-Tier Death Trap**

The place where firms are actually in the most trouble is not at the extremes—not the giants with distribution moats, and not the tiny AI-native teams. It’s the middle tier of professional services and software.

The data is starting to show this clearly. Forrester analysis suggests agency headcounts have fallen 8% in 2025. The major holding companies are all cutting: Dentsu eliminated 3,400 jobs (8% of staff), WPP cut 7,000 during the final months of Mark Read’s CEO tenure, Interpublic Group laid off 3,200 employees, and Omnicom cut 3,000 jobs in 2024 ahead of its merger with IPG. GroupM is restructuring nearly half of its U.S. headcount. Bureau of Labor Statistics data shows the American advertising industry lost 4,600 roles between August and December 2024 alone.

But here’s the crucial detail: the giants are cutting to get leaner, not because they’re dying. They’re consolidating, restructuring, positioning for an AI-native future. Meanwhile, the mid-tier firms—too small to have distribution moats, too large to be lean AI-native teams—are in genuine distress. R/GA, once a celebrated independent agency, laid off 15% of its staff and sold to a private equity firm. The layoffs in the advertising industry aren’t evenly distributed; they’re concentrated in the squeezable middle.

Consider a marketing agency with fifty employees. For fifteen years, the firm made money by being professional, reliable, and competent. Not the cheapest, but they delivered solid work on time and built client relationships. What does this firm actually sell? Mostly first-layer work—plans, campaigns, copy, strategy decks. The partners did some second-layer work in client management and accountability, but the bulk of revenue was billed to cognitive production.

AI changes this firm’s competitive position from multiple directions. From below, a team of three people with good AI tools can produce comparable volume. The work isn’t identical, but it’s close enough that clients start wondering why they’re paying for fifty people. The tiny team is faster, cheaper, and increasingly competitive on quality. From above, giant holding companies have distribution moats, end-to-end offerings, and embedded ecosystems that the fifty-person agency cannot hope to match.

The mid-tier agency is stuck. It can’t match the cost structure of lean AI-native competitors because it has employees, offices, and overhead from a different era. It also can’t match the distribution and stickiness the giants bring. This dynamic is playing out across the entire digital professional services economy—software consultancies, design firms, analytics shops, content production companies, research firms. Any mid-tier player whose value proposition was “we produce cognitive work” is in danger.

This is precisely what makes the Cognition-Infosys example instructive. Infosys isn’t mid-tier in IT services—they’re a distribution giant in their domain. The firms getting squeezed are the mid-sized software consultancies that have neither Cognition’s cutting-edge AI capability nor Infosys’s relationship infrastructure. They’re competing on “we produce solid code,” which is exactly the first-layer value proposition that’s collapsing. They can’t partner with Cognition the way Infosys can, because they don’t bring distribution to the table. And they can’t build Devin themselves. The middle is where you find firms with some capability and some relationships, but not enough of either to survive the squeeze.


## **Investment Implications by Position**

Given these dynamics, AI investment strategy depends heavily on where a firm sits in the economy.

**For mid-tier firms in contestable digital markets**—marketing, software services, design, content, analytics—there are two viable paths and one death trap.

The first path is to get radically lean: cut headcount, cut overhead, rebuild around a small core team that uses AI to produce at the level your current staff produces. You’re racing against three-person startups and need to act accordingly.

The second path is to move up the stack: stop selling first-layer cognitive production and lean heavily into second-layer judgment, accountability, and quality. This means changing what you charge for—not deliverables, but the quality of decision-making. AI investments should support that shift by helping senior people do more high-judgment work, which is different from helping junior people produce more drafts.

The death trap is investing in AI to make your current model more efficient. If your model is fifty people producing cognitive work and you make that twenty percent more efficient, it’s probably not enough to compete with super-lean players. You’re just dying slower.

**For firms in physical, local, relationship-heavy markets**—trades, dentistry, professional services with high switching costs—AI investments should focus on back-office efficiency. Scheduling, dispatch optimization, automated customer communications, quoting, invoicing, collections. This isn’t glamorous transformation; it’s overhead reduction that lets you focus on the third-layer work where you actually compete.

These firms should be wary of vendors selling strategic transformation when what they need is basic efficiency. There’s no point in becoming “AI transformed” if AI isn’t going to change what you deliver and you’re a plumber. You’ve got to just do the plumbing. The opportunity is to do the plumbing with less administrative drag and better margins—not to reinvent your business model.

**For AI-native startups**, the Cognition playbook is worth studying carefully. Pure cognitive production is a depreciating asset. If your startup’s value proposition is “we use AI to produce X cheaper and faster,” you’re in the commodity business. Every other AI-native startup can make the same claim. Your margins will compress as underlying models get cheaper. This can still be a good business if you have distribution, if you’re embedded in workflows, if switching costs are high—but cognitive production alone is not a moat.

The question for any AI startup is: do you go direct to customers, or do you sell through incumbents who have distribution? Going direct means long sales cycles, relationship-building you may not have the capital to fund, and competing against entrenched vendors on trust rather than capability. Selling through incumbents—the Cognition path—means instant distribution, zero relationship building, entire go-to-market cycles skipped. The trade-off is that you’re giving up margin and direct customer relationships to the distribution partner.

This is why the startups that will sustain value long-term are those that own bottlenecks in the second layer: compliance integration, audit infrastructure, human-in-the-loop review systems, liability and accountability wrappers around agents, workflow orchestration that embeds you in how clients operate. These are harder to build than “AI that makes X,” but they’re far more defensible—and they’re the assets that make you a valuable partner rather than a replaceable capability provider.

**For giants with real distribution moats**—embedded client relationships, bundled offerings, platform status, brand recognition—AI is upside opportunity. The moat isn’t automatically eroded by AI; if anything, it becomes more valuable as production commoditizes and distribution becomes the scarce resource. Infosys understood this: they didn’t try to build Devin; they partnered with the people who did. The capability comes to them because they have something capability providers need.

But the risk for giants is internal. The threat isn’t that startups will eat you directly; it’s that they’ll hire away your best people by offering speed and upside. The threat is that you won’t change how you operate fast enough, and startups will nibble at the edges of your empire. Think of AI as increasing the speed of all the ankle-biters in your space. Your job is to invest on your internal talent side enough that you can encourage innovation at a pace that allows you to match those threats and defend the space your business currently occupies.

AI investment for giants should be coupled with genuine operational change. You’re investing in your teams. You’re convincing your teams that they’re in the right place, that you care about them, that they have an AI-native future inside this larger business and that you’re going to give them the tools they need to build amazing things. The internal innovation challenge is often overlooked. Firms that lose long-term are typically those that lose the ability to deliver extraordinary service—they get out-competed by firms that execute the core promise more efficiently over time.


## **What This Implies for Traditional Industries**

Traditional industries do not fail because they “don’t have AI.” They fail because they can’t change their operating model.

And that’s where the management and dynamism literature becomes relevant. The U.S. economy has shown declining business dynamism over recent decades, including declining entrepreneurship and young-firm activity, and those dynamics interact with productivity and reallocation.

AI is a new general-purpose change shock landing on a system that already has sluggish reallocation and persistent management dispersion. That implies winners will be the firms that keep structured management practices and re-architect workflows (and survive the J-curve). Losers will be the firms that bolt AI onto legacy processes, absorb the disruption costs, and never reach the upside.

This is why “AI adoption” and “AI advantage” are not the same thing. Adoption is cheap. Advantage is organizational.


## **A Complete Picture**

If I had to state the thesis on one page:

AI is collapsing the marginal cost of cognition, which triggers Jevons expansion (firms do more, not less) and intensifies Baumol dynamics by making the remaining human bottlenecks—accountability, trust, regulation, and embodied service—more valuable and more expensive.

In an economy where firm productivity dispersion is already large and persistent, and where management quality varies widely, AI does not produce instant convergence. It produces a widening gap between firms that can re-architect workflows and those that cannot.

Firm death rises most sharply in markets that become more contestable and substitutable as AI commoditizes baseline cognitive work, while fragmentation persists (and prices rise) in local, relationship-heavy, capacity-constrained markets where AI reduces overhead but cannot substitute for embodied scarcity.

The underappreciated kill mechanism in 2025 is the adoption J-curve: short-term disruption and reorganization costs cause some firms to fail before they ever realize the productivity upside, especially among older firms and those with weaker operating discipline—and whether those firms actually exit depends heavily on capital conditions and the extent to which zombie dynamics delay cleansing.

The economy that emerges is not simply “the long tail survives” or “the long tail dies.” It’s consolidation in digital cognition markets (long tails die), persistent fragmentation in local embodied markets (long tails persist and charge more), and a squeeze and shakeout in the middle driven by the adoption valley, rising baseline expectations, and capital pressure.

The Cognition-Infosys dynamic offers a preview of how this plays out in practice. AI capability flows toward distribution. Distribution moats become more valuable, not less. The middle tier—firms with some capability and some relationships but not enough of either—finds itself without a dance partner. The expected disruption is happening, but not in the direction most observers predicted. The giants aren’t falling to AI-native startups. They’re absorbing them.

## **What to Expect in 2026**

If the framework in this article is correct, you should see specific patterns emerge over the next twelve to eighteen months. Here’s what I expect:

**Mid-tier professional services will show accelerating consolidation.** Marketing agencies, software consultancies, design firms, and analytics shops in the 20-100 employee range will see elevated closure rates, merger activity, and distressed acquisitions. The survivors will be noticeably smaller (the lean path) or noticeably more specialized in high-judgment work (the move-up-the-stack path). Firms that tried to “add AI” to their existing model without structural change will disproportionately fail.

**AI startup exits will route through incumbents, not around them.** The Cognition-Infosys pattern will become the norm, not the exception. Expect to see more AI capability companies partnering with or selling to traditional enterprises rather than displacing them. The startups that insist on going direct will burn more capital on sales cycles and show slower growth than those that plug into existing distribution. The “AI disintermediates the middleman” thesis will look increasingly naive by year-end.

**Local services pricing will rise faster than inflation.** Plumbers, electricians, HVAC technicians, dentists, and other embodied-service providers will raise prices, and customers will pay. This will confuse observers expecting AI to make everything cheaper. The Baumol mechanism is straightforward: as AI-augmented sectors bid up wages, labor-constrained services get more expensive. Watch the CPI components for services that require physical presence.

**Enterprise AI spending will consolidate around fewer vendors with more comprehensive offerings.** The long tail of point-solution AI tools will struggle. Enterprises don’t want seventeen AI products; they want two or three that integrate with existing workflows. Distribution advantage will eat capability advantage. Procurement will favor bundled offerings from known vendors over best-of-breed from unknowns.

**The productivity stats will remain disappointing at the macro level.** Despite high adoption rates, aggregate productivity growth will underwhelm relative to expectations. This isn’t because AI doesn’t work. It’s because we’re in the J-curve valley where reorganization costs dominate and most firms haven’t yet reached the upside. Expect continued headlines about the “productivity paradox” through at least mid-2026.

**The talent war will intensify for second-layer skills.** As first-layer cognitive production commoditizes, the premium on judgment, taste, and accountability will become more visible in compensation data. Senior people who can evaluate AI output, make high-stakes decisions, and own outcomes will command higher salaries. The gap between “can prompt AI effectively” and “can be accountable for AI-assisted decisions” will widen in market value.

**The giants will not fall.** Expect continued hand-wringing about whether large enterprises are “moving fast enough on AI.” They will largely be fine. Their distribution moats, embedded relationships, and accountability infrastructure are more valuable as capability commoditizes, not less. The threat to giants remains internal—talent retention and organizational ossification—not external disruption from startups.

If these predictions are wrong, the framework needs revision. If they’re right, you’ll have had a twelve-month head start on repositioning.
