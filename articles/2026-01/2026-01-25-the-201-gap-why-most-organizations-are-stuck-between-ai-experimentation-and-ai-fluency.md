# **The 201 Gap: Why Most Organizations Are Stuck Between AI Experimentation and AI Fluency**


## **The Productivity Paradox**

The productivity gains from AI are documented. The tools are deployed. And yet most organizations aren’t capturing the value—because they’ve invested in the wrong layer.

A February 2025 St. Louis Fed study found workers are 33% more productive in hours using generative AI. A Harvard/BCG field experiment showed 40% improvement in quality, 25.1% faster completion, 12.2% more tasks done. These aren’t cherry-picked wins—they’re reproducible findings from rigorous research. But the same BCG study revealed a troubling flip side: when consultants used AI on tasks outside its capability frontier, they were 19 percentage points *less likely* to produce correct solutions than those working without AI at all.

The tool that makes good judgment better makes poor judgment catastrophically worse.

This explains the enterprise stall: over 80% of organizations have explored ChatGPT and Copilot, but only 5% made it to production deployment. Gartner predicts 30% of generative AI projects will be abandoned after proof of concept by end of 2025. The technology works. The capability to use it reliably doesn’t exist in most workforces.

The missing layer is what I call the 201 skills—the applied judgment that turns AI tools into consistent productivity gains. Training programs have bifurcated into 101 basics (tool tours, prompting fundamentals) and 401 technical implementation (APIs, RAG, fine-tuning), while almost entirely skipping the middle. Knowledge workers get demos, then they’re left to figure out the hard part on their own. Most don’t.

**This briefing covers:**

- **The productivity paradox.** Why documented AI gains aren’t translating to enterprise results—and how the “jagged frontier” between AI capability and incapability creates invisible failure modes.
- **What the 201 gap actually is.** The specific capability layer between basic tool usage and technical implementation that determines whether AI adoption succeeds or stalls.
- **Why the gap persists.** Five structural reasons—from training bifurcation to the collapse of apprenticeship models—that won’t resolve themselves.
- **What closing the gap looks like.** The Centaur and Cyborg patterns that distinguish effective AI users, with concrete before/after comparisons.
- **The six meta-skills.** A durable framework for 201 capabilities—context assembly, quality judgment, task decomposition, iterative refinement, workflow integration, frontier recognition—with specific interventions for each.
- **201 in practice.** A library of use cases across functions showing what 201-level work actually looks like, from financial analysis to healthcare handoffs.
- **The director assessment.** Five questions to diagnose where your organization actually stands, plus organizational approaches that work.

The fix isn’t complicated—but it requires seeing exactly where the breakdown happens. It starts with a question most organizations skip: what’s actually in the 201 layer, and why does training keep missing it?


## **What the 201 Gap Actually Is**

Think about how most organizations approach AI skill-building. At the 101 level, you have the basics: tool tours, prompting fundamentals, generic use cases. This is the “here’s what ChatGPT can do” material that floods corporate training programs. It teaches people that AI exists and roughly how to interact with it. Essential, but insufficient.

At the 401 level, you have the technical implementation: API integration, RAG architectures, LangChain, fine-tuning, multi-agent orchestration. This is the province of developers, AI engineers, and platform teams. They’re building AI systems, not using AI tools. Critical for organizations that want to scale, but not relevant for most knowledge workers.

The 201 level sits between these, and it’s where the productivity gains actually live—or die. At 201, the question shifts from “how do I use this tool?” to “where does this tool fit in my actual workflow, and how do I know when its output is trustworthy?” This is the applied judgment layer. It’s not about writing better prompts. It’s about knowing which parts of your work AI should do, which parts you should do, and how to verify the handoffs.

The gap exists because training has bifurcated into those two poles—101 basics and 401-level technical implementation—while almost entirely skipping the middle. Knowledge workers get tool demos and generic prompting courses, then they’re left to figure out the hard part on their own. Most of them don’t. They try the tool a few times, get mediocre results, and quietly stop using it while still claiming on surveys that they “use AI regularly.”


## **Why the Gap Exists**

The 201 gap persists for structural reasons that won’t resolve themselves. Understanding these causes is the first step toward closing the gap in your organization.

**Training has bifurcated into 101 and 401-level, skipping the middle.** The market is flooded with “AI fundamentals” courses that teach prompting basics and “AI engineering” courses that teach technical implementation. The applied workflow layer for knowledge workers—the actual skill that generates the documented productivity gains—barely exists as a training category. Organizations buy what’s available, and what’s available doesn’t address the real capability gap.

**The skills that matter aren’t prompting skills—they’re management skills.** Ethan Mollick puts it directly: “The best users of AI I know are good managers, are good teachers. The skills that make you good at AI are not prompting skills, they’re people skills. If you’re good at understanding what someone might be confused about, if you’re good at breaking down tasks into steps, if you’re good at troubleshooting when someone goes wrong, you’re going to be good at AI.”

This insight reframes the entire problem. The 201 skill set isn’t technical—it’s about task decomposition, quality assessment, iterative refinement, and knowing when to trust versus verify. These are judgment skills, not tool skills. And they’re exactly the skills that corporate training programs systematically under-invest in because they’re hard to measure and don’t fit neatly into vendor-provided curriculum.

**Generic tools excel for individuals but stall at enterprise scale.** ChatGPT, Claude, and Copilot are remarkably flexible. That flexibility is their strength for individual use and their weakness for enterprise deployment. As MIT research identifies, most GenAI systems do not retain feedback, adapt to context, or improve over time. Every interaction starts from zero. This means the productivity gains that individual power users achieve don’t automatically transfer to their teammates, their successors, or their organizations. The learning stays locked in individual heads.

**IT departments are building the wrong things.** “A lot of banks are turning towards their IT department and saying: ‘do the AI thing,’” Mollick observes. “But there’s no reason the IT groups should be experts on what the AI thing is. It doesn’t work like code, it works like a person. They build the same four or five solutions every time.” The result is a mismatch between what IT builds and what business users actually need. IT focuses on infrastructure and security—important, but not the capability gap. Business users need workflow integration and judgment frameworks—critical, but not what IT typically delivers.

**The “jagged frontier” problem makes expertise essential.** The BCG research documented what they call the “jagged frontier”—the irregular boundary between what AI does well and what it does poorly. That boundary doesn’t follow intuitive lines. AI can write a compelling marketing email but can’t reliably extract the right numbers from a financial statement. It can synthesize research across dozens of sources but might confidently cite papers that don’t exist. The frontier isn’t just jagged; it’s invisible to users who haven’t mapped it through experience. This is why the BCG study found that experts showed lower productivity gains from AI (8-13% vs. 40% for non-experts) but also made far fewer AI-induced errors. The experts already knew where the boundaries were. They had the judgment to avoid the traps. That judgment is the 201 skill.

**The apprenticeship model is collapsing.** “As managers and employees use AI to automate lower-level tasks, it’s collapsing the apprenticeship model that underpins talent development,” notes recent research on AI’s impact on professional development. “That training pipeline that was always implicit has broken, and it has to be reconstructed at the CHRO level.” Junior employees used to develop judgment by doing the routine work that’s now delegated to AI. If organizations don’t intentionally rebuild that developmental pathway, they’ll face a judgment deficit in their workforce that compounds over time.


## **What the Gap Looks Like (And Feels Like)**

Abstract discussions of the 201 gap don’t capture what it actually feels like when an organization is stuck in it. Here’s what the gap looks like from the inside.

**The Three-Week Dip.** Microsoft’s internal data shows a consistent pattern: initial delight and experimentation in the first three weeks of usage, a dip in enthusiasm from weeks three through 10, and then more consistent usage around the 11-week mark for those who persist. Most organizations lose people in that trough. They tried it, it didn’t “work,” they stopped. What “didn’t work” actually means is revealing: Whatfix research (vendor observation, but directionally useful) found that most employees default to vague, generic inputs that return shallow results. After a few weak experiences, trust erodes and usage drops. The employee tried “Help me with this report,” got something unusable, tried again, got something worse, and concluded the tool is overhyped.

**The guesswork problem.** Organizations that skip defining role-specific use cases force users to guess where AI fits. Guesswork kills adoption. This is the 201 gap in its purest form: people know AI exists, they have access to it, they just don’t know where it goes in their actual work.

From the employee’s perspective, the gap feels like frustration and abandonment: “I know everyone says this is transformative, but every time I try it I just end up rewriting the whole thing anyway.” Or: “It’s faster to just do it myself than to explain what I need.” Or: “I asked it for help with my quarterly analysis and it gave me something that sounds confident but is completely wrong. I can’t trust it.”

From the manager’s perspective, it feels like an expensive mystery: “I bought everyone licenses six months ago. Usage dashboards show 20% monthly active. I have no idea what the other 80% are doing—or why they stopped.” Research captures this perfectly: “Everyone has their own business goals and metrics they need to hit, and they need to know how Copilot will specifically improve their lives.” But nobody has shown them.

**The opposite failure mode: falling asleep at the wheel.** The BCG/Harvard research documented what happens when people trust AI too much on the wrong tasks. Dell’Acqua found that recruiters who used high-quality AI became lazy and made worse decisions than those who relied on their own judgment. The AI was so good on some tasks that people stopped checking on all tasks. Human consultants got the problem right 84% of the time without AI help, but when consultants used the AI, they did worse—only getting it right 60-70% of the time. This isn’t an AI failure. It’s a judgment failure. The 201 skill is knowing when to trust and when to verify—and people who lack it either never trust (and abandon the tool) or always trust (and make costly errors).

**The fear pattern.** One more experiential element that often gets overlooked: fear of “doing it wrong” stops more AI adoption than any other factor. People need clear guardrails. What data can we use? When must we disclose AI assistance? What happens if the AI makes a mistake? Without this clarity, talented people will simply avoid AI entirely. The 201 gap isn’t just a skill gap—it’s often a permission gap. People don’t know if they’re allowed to use it, what’s safe to paste in, whether they’ll get in trouble if the AI makes a mistake. The absence of organizational guidance creates paralysis.


## **What Closing the Gap Looks Like**

The same BCG study shows what happens when people operate inside the AI’s capability frontier with good judgment: consultants using AI finished 12.2% more tasks on average, completed tasks 25.1% more quickly, and produced 40% higher quality results than those without.

The researchers identified two distinctive patterns among successful AI users. One set of consultants acted as “Centaurs,” dividing and delegating their solution-creation activities to the AI or to themselves. They treated AI as a collaborator with distinct responsibilities. Another set acted more like “Cyborgs,” completely integrating their task flow with the AI and continually interacting with the technology. The boundary between human and AI work became fluid.

Both patterns work—but they require knowing where the boundaries are. That knowledge is the 201 skill.

The Centaur experience feels like running a relay race. You know exactly where to hand off. “I’ll do the strategy framing, have AI generate the option set, then I’ll evaluate and pick.” The employee stops thinking of AI as “a tool I use sometimes” and starts thinking of it as “how I work now.”

The Cyborg experience feels like the boundary dissolving. You’re drafting and prompting simultaneously, refining in real-time, never quite sure where your thinking ends and the AI’s begins. This works for creative and iterative tasks where continuous interaction improves the output.

From the employee’s perspective, closing the gap sounds like this: “It’s like having a really fast research assistant who never gets tired. I still have to know what questions to ask and whether the answers make sense—but the searching and drafting part that used to take hours now takes minutes.” Or: “I realized the skill isn’t writing better prompts—it’s breaking my work into pieces and knowing which pieces the AI is actually good at.”

From the manager’s perspective, it sounds like transformation: “We went from ‘some people use it sometimes’ to ‘this is just how we do RFP responses now.’ We have a playbook. New hires learn it in onboarding.” One leader described a turning point during a team meeting when a typically quiet junior employee demonstrated how AI had optimized their workflow, improving efficiency by 30%. “That was my wake-up call.”

## **A Durable Framework for the 201 Skills**

Here’s the key insight from SFIA, the global skills framework for technology professionals: effective AI skill frameworks describe professional capabilities for working with AI without prescribing specific technologies. They focus on enduring capabilities adaptable to any AI platform or tool.

This matters because the 201 skills that generate productivity gains aren’t about ChatGPT or Claude or Copilot. They’re about judgment capabilities that transfer across tools and survive model upgrades. When the next major model releases, when new tools emerge, when your organization switches platforms—the 201 skills remain relevant.

These are the six meta-skills that constitute the 201 layer:

**1. Context Assembly**

This is knowing what information to provide, from which sources, and why. Research identifies this as the biggest gap: knowledge workers struggle to synthesize information from a variety of sources, a challenge compounded by the tacit nature of domain expertise required to effectively analyze and compare data.

At 101, people either dump entire documents into AI or provide almost no context. Both approaches produce mediocre results. The 101 user thinks the AI will figure it out. The 201 user understands that AI is sensitive to context quality—the right background, constraints, and examples dramatically improve output quality.

Context assembly requires information triage: distinguishing what’s essential from what’s reference from what’s noise. It requires understanding what the AI needs to know versus what you need to know versus what neither of you needs. This is a learnable skill, but it requires practice with real multi-document workflows, not abstract prompting exercises.

**2. Quality Judgment**

This is knowing when to trust AI output and when to verify it. The BCG study showed that experts don’t get much productivity gain (8-13%) but also don’t make as many AI-induced errors. The skill they have is knowing when the AI is wrong.

Quality judgment operates on two dimensions. First, knowing which task types require what level of verification—high-stakes legal and financial work gets scrutinized; low-stakes drafts get light editing. Second, knowing within a given output which parts are likely reliable and which parts are likely problematic. AI confidently states both accurate information and hallucinations in the same paragraph. The 201 skill is detecting which is which.

Developing quality judgment requires explicit verification protocols by task type, not generic “always check the AI’s work” guidance. It requires building organizational knowledge of where AI excels versus fails for your specific work, and sharing failure cases explicitly so the whole team learns from each mistake.

**3. Task Decomposition**

This is breaking work into AI-appropriate chunks rather than throwing whole tasks at the tool or avoiding it entirely. The Centaur and Cyborg patterns both require this—knowing which subtasks to delegate versus do yourself.

Effective task decomposition maps existing workflows to AI-appropriate subtasks and identifies “Centaur points” where handoff makes sense. It requires understanding that some parts of your work are well-suited to AI (research, drafting, pattern identification, format conversion) while other parts require human judgment (strategy, stakeholder relationships, ethical decisions, final quality).

The 201 practitioner thinks of AI as “my proposal writing intern”—a framing that sets appropriate expectations for oversight and collaboration. The 101 user thinks of AI as either a magic solution (disappointment follows) or a useless toy (abandonment follows).

**4. Iterative Refinement**

This is the skill of moving from 70% to 95% through structured passes. The 101 user accepts the first output or abandons it entirely. The 201 user treats the first draft as a starting point.

Mollick’s framework captures this well: give role, goal, and guardrails; ask for an outline first; iterate with pointed edits; you own the final fact-check and style pass. This is the “intern model” of AI collaboration—you wouldn’t accept an intern’s first draft without feedback and revision, and you shouldn’t accept AI’s first draft either.

Iterative refinement requires building iteration into the expected workflow rather than treating AI output as final. It requires specific feedback skills: knowing how to guide AI toward improvement rather than starting over from scratch with each unsatisfactory result.

**5. Workflow Integration**

This is embedding AI into how work actually gets done rather than treating it as a side tool. The difference shows up in whether AI is a separate activity (”I’ll try the AI thing later”) or an integrated capability (”this is how we do RFP responses now”).

Workflow integration requires redesigning actual processes to include AI steps, not just providing access to AI tools. Where possible, it means embedding AI in existing tools—meeting summaries that automatically flow to project management, document analysis that feeds directly into reporting templates, communication drafts that pull from approved content libraries.

The research finding is clear: employees who receive more than five hours of formal AI training are 12 percentage points more likely to become regular users. But training alone isn’t enough—the AI has to fit into how people actually work, not require them to switch to a new application and back again.

**6. Frontier Recognition**

This is knowing when you’re operating outside the AI’s capability boundary. This is the skill that prevents the 19-percentage-point performance drop when AI is used on tasks it can’t handle.

Frontier recognition requires building explicit organizational knowledge of where AI excels versus fails for your specific work. It requires sharing failure cases so the whole team learns the boundaries, not just the individuals who encountered them. It requires intellectual humility—understanding that AI’s capabilities don’t match human intuitions about what should be easy or hard.

The jagged frontier isn’t static. It shifts with new models, new tools, new training. But the skill of recognizing the frontier—staying alert to where you might be outside AI’s reliable zone—remains constant across all of them.

## **201 in Practice: What It Actually Looks Like**

Abstract skill frameworks matter, but directors need to see what 201 work looks like in practice. Here’s a library of 201-level use cases organized by function, showing both the what and the how that distinguish 201 from basic tool usage.


#### **Information Synthesis and Research**

*Multi-Source Briefing.* An analyst needs to prepare an executive briefing on a competitor announcement. At 101, they’d paste an article into ChatGPT and ask for a summary. At 201, they work in structured phases: research phase using Perplexity to gather current market data, competitor analyses, and trend information; analysis phase using Claude to upload all research and request strategic synthesis; creation phase using ChatGPT to translate strategy into concrete content. The 201 skill is knowing which tool for which subtask and assembling context from multiple sources before asking for synthesis.

*Document Comparison.* A legal team needs to compare contract versions or expert depositions. At 101, they’d ask “what’s different between these two documents?” At 201, they use AI to compare prior expert depositions, identify gaps in expert reasoning, and surface patterns across large document sets—then apply human review for accuracy before using the analysis in case strategy. The 201 skill is using AI to identify patterns while maintaining verification protocols for high-stakes work.

*Literature Review.* A researcher needs to synthesize findings across multiple papers. At 201, they match tool to task type: Claude for literature review because its ability to maintain theoretical frameworks across long documents is unmatched; ChatGPT with Code Interpreter for data analysis because the ability to write, execute, and iterate on Python code in real-time transforms exploratory analysis. The 201 skill is using large context windows to maintain coherence across sources while matching tool capability to task requirements.


#### **Meeting Workflow**

*Meeting Catch-Up.* An executive returns from PTO with 20 meetings to catch up on. At 101, they’d read through all transcripts manually or ask AI to summarize them one by one. At 201, they query across meetings: “What did I miss in the meetings last week?”—and get a full update on key decisions, actions, and mentions of their name. The 201 skill is knowing to query across meetings and understanding what AI can and can’t reliably extract.

*Meeting-to-Action Pipeline.* Weekly team meetings need to flow into project management. At 201, AI generates summaries, human reviews action items, and the system automatically sends notes to Confluence and converts action items into Jira work items. The 201 skill is connecting AI output to downstream systems—building the handoff into the workflow rather than treating AI as a side tool.

*Shift Handoff.* Hospitals, operations centers, and 24/7 environments need clean handoffs. At 201, AI meeting summaries standardize shift-change communication. HCA Healthcare is piloting an AI-powered handoff app across five hospitals; early feedback from one facility rated summaries 86% factual and 90% helpful. The current process takes nurses an average of 40 minutes per shift—10 million hours annually across HCA’s system. The 201 skill is standardizing format and structure so AI-generated summaries are consistent and actionable across shifts.


#### **Proposal and RFP Response**

*RFP First Draft.* A 100-question security questionnaire arrives with 48-hour turnaround. At 101, you’d answer questions one by one with AI assistance. At 201, you start with AI Agents for the heavy lift—reviewing RFPs, producing 75%-ready drafts, and filling large questionnaires. Then you switch to AI Tools to finish: polish, tighten, and ensure compliance. The 201 skill is using AI for structure (the 80% draft) while reserving human time for strategy—win themes, differentiation, compliance review.

*Content Library Leverage.* Recurring questions appear across multiple RFPs. At 201, AI draws from a trusted, curated content library with vetted answers. Tools like Loopio centralize responses so teams can quickly find approved language. The 201 skill is building and maintaining a trusted content library that AI can draw from, and knowing when to use library content versus generate fresh responses.

*SME Collaboration.* You need technical input from busy subject matter experts. At 201, AI creates scaffolding that SMEs can react to rather than asking them to create from scratch. “ChatGPT can take an answer 80% of the way there. Whenever a SME doesn’t have to start with a blank page, it’s always well-received.” The 201 skill is using AI to reduce SME burden while maintaining technical accuracy.


#### **Financial Analysis and Reporting**

*Variance Analysis.* Monthly close requires explaining budget vs. actual variances. At 201, a financial analyst inputs data and instantly receives comprehensive analysis, saving several hours of manual work. Then human review applies domain expertise to verify accuracy and add strategic interpretation. The 201 skill is using AI to identify patterns and generate first-pass explanations while applying domain expertise to verify and contextualize.

*Financial Summarization.* Materials need to be prepared for board or leadership. At 201, teams swiftly summarize financial statements, contracts, and emails using AI. The 201 skill is knowing what level of detail different audiences need and using AI for translation between technical and executive formats.

*Trend Identification.* Analyzing spending patterns across departments. At 101, you create pivot tables manually. At 201, you use AI to pull financial data from multiple sheets, identify patterns, and generate summaries for leadership meetings. The 201 skill is using AI to surface patterns, then applying business judgment to determine which patterns matter.


#### **HR and Talent**

*Job Description Creation.* A new role needs a clear, accurate job description. At 201, AI drafts based on required skills, qualifications, and responsibilities, pulling from existing HR documents to ensure consistency and accuracy. The 201 skill is providing AI with existing organizational standards and templates while reviewing for compliance with employment law.

*Resume Screening.* 200 applications for one role. At 201, AI scans resumes, extracts key skills and experience, and organizes them into easy-to-review summaries. The 201 skill is defining screening criteria before AI involvement and understanding bias risks in automated screening.

*Onboarding Acceleration.* A new hire needs to ramp on complex project history. At 201, you build an “onboarding digest” from past project summaries. The 201 skill is curating which historical content to include and ensuring AI doesn’t surface outdated or sensitive information.


#### **Content and Communications**

*Executive Communications.* The CEO needs a weekly all-hands update. At 201, you match AI tool to communication type—Claude for formal emails, SOPs, training documents, and technical documentation where clarity and precision are paramount. The 201 skill is maintaining voice consistency and applying human review for accuracy and tone.

*Multi-Channel Adaptation.* One announcement needs email, Slack, and LinkedIn versions. At 201, AI generates drafts from notes, and interactive editing allows real-time refinement of tone and complexity. The 201 skill is using AI to adapt format and length for different channels while maintaining message consistency.

*Marketing Content Velocity.* Need five ad variations by end of day. At 201, AI generates creative visualizations, saving up to 30 minutes per day for employees doing this work. Dentsu reports these gains across hundreds of employees. The 201 skill is using AI for variants and iterations while applying human judgment to brand alignment and creative direction.


#### **Technical and Operations**

*IT Support Triage.* 50 helpdesk tickets daily, many routine. At 201, AI addresses up to 40% of helpdesk demands automatically. PG&E has clocked annual savings of more than $1 million. The 201 skill is identifying which ticket types can be handled automatically versus require human expertise.

*Code Review and Documentation.* A developer needs to document legacy code or review a PR. At 201, AI integration enhances coding accuracy and reduces daily communications overhead by 50%. The 201 skill is using AI for documentation generation while maintaining security protocols for proprietary code.

*Process Documentation.* An SOP needs to be created or updated. At 201, AI drafts documentation following organizational templates and standards. The 201 skill is providing AI with existing templates and verifying accuracy of process steps with subject matter experts.


#### **The Pattern Across All Use Cases**

What distinguishes 201 from 101 in every case comes down to five consistent elements:

*Context Assembly.* The skilled user knows what background, constraints, and examples to provide before asking AI to generate anything.

*Task Decomposition.* They break work into AI-appropriate chunks rather than throwing whole tasks at the tool. Framing AI as “my proposal writing intern” sets expectations for oversight and collaboration.

*Verification Protocol.* They know which outputs require what level of review. Legal and financial work gets scrutinized; low-stakes drafts get light editing.

*Workflow Integration.* AI isn’t a separate activity—it’s embedded in how work actually flows. Meeting notes automatically become Jira tickets. Research automatically feeds analysis.

*Tool Selection.* They match tool to task type. Platform specialization beats all-in-one approaches—ChatGPT excels at creative brainstorming, Claude dominates analytical work and enterprise workflows, Perplexity owns research and fact-checking with citation transparency.

Understanding the 201 gap requires seeing how it fits into the broader landscape of AI capability. Here’s how the three levels compare:

The progression path between levels isn’t automatic.

**101 → 201** happens through practice, good examples, and intentional skill-building. The gap closes when workers develop judgment about where AI fits, not just how to use it. This transition requires deliberate effort but doesn’t require organizational decision-making—individuals and teams can make it happen.

**201 → 401** happens through organizational decision-making. Someone has to decide to build rather than just use. This requires executive sponsorship for infrastructure investment, technical resources (internal or partner), data readiness (clean, accessible, governed), and change management for new ways of working.

The recommendation from experienced practitioners: start with Vanilla RAG for initial deployments, then evolve the architecture based on proven ROI and organizational needs. This phased approach minimizes risk while building internal capabilities for advanced implementations.


## **The Director+ Assessment**

For senior leaders, the question isn’t “which level should we be at?” but “which level does each function need to operate at?”

Every knowledge worker needs 201 skills. That’s table stakes. Power users in each function should be identifying 401 opportunities—workflows that could be systematized, not just assisted. Central teams (IT, AI CoE, ops) should be building 401 infrastructure that 201 users can leverage.

The assessment question for any director isn’t “do my people use AI?” It’s five more specific questions:

**Can they identify which subtasks AI should do versus they should do?** This is task decomposition. If your people are throwing whole tasks at AI or avoiding it entirely, they’re stuck at 101.

**Do they have explicit verification protocols for different risk levels?** This is quality judgment. If your people either accept everything AI produces or reject everything, they’re stuck at 101.

**Are they iterating, or accepting first outputs?** This is iterative refinement. If your people treat AI output as final rather than as a starting point for revision, they’re stuck at 101.

**Has AI been integrated into actual workflows, or is it a side activity?** This is workflow integration. If your people have to switch to a different application to use AI and then switch back, they’re stuck at 101.

**Do they know—specifically, for your work—where the AI fails?** This is frontier recognition. If your people can’t tell you the tasks where AI consistently makes mistakes in your domain, they’re stuck at 101.

The organizations pulling ahead aren’t just training people to prompt better. They’re building the systems that make AI work automatically at scale. Yet despite growth in AI capability and investment, only 2% of organizations have deployed agentic AI at scale, while 61% remain stuck in exploration phases.

The 201 layer is what separates exploration from production. It’s what turns AI tools into reliable productivity gains. And it’s what most organizations are missing.


## **Closing the Gap: Organizational Approaches**

The research points to specific interventions that work:

**Create AI labs with power users, not just technologists.** Labs should be lightweight, fast-moving teams experimenting with workflows, roles, and learning journeys. Importantly, labs should include your AI power users—often employees without technical backgrounds who’ve figured out real use cases. They know where AI actually adds value in your organization. Technologists know how to build; power users know what to build.

**Conduct systematic discovery across functions.** Don’t assume you know where AI fits. Trek Bicycle interviewed every department about how AI might improve their work, and employees at all levels were asked to contribute feedback. From these interviews, the team presented senior managers with nearly 40 concrete use cases for AI. Your organization has similar hidden knowledge—but you have to surface it deliberately.

**Make success visible.** Run low-stakes competitions: “What’s one workflow you’ve meaningfully improved using AI?” Offer rewards, visibility, and cross-team collaboration time. This surfaces practical applications, creates organizational learning, and builds social proof that encourages experimentation.

**Invest in hours, not just access.** Employees who receive more than five hours of formal AI training are 12 percentage points more likely to become regular users. The gap between tool rollout and actual adoption remains wide—but it’s fixable. The investment isn’t expensive compared to the productivity gains at stake.

**Define guardrails explicitly.** Fear of “doing it wrong” stops more AI adoption than any other factor. What data can we use? When must we disclose AI assistance? What happens if AI makes a mistake? Without this clarity, talented people will avoid AI entirely. Clear guardrails enable experimentation rather than constraining it.

**Share failure cases systematically.** The jagged frontier only becomes visible through experience. When someone discovers a task that AI handles poorly, that knowledge needs to spread across the organization—not stay locked in one person’s head. Create mechanisms for sharing what doesn’t work, not just what does.

**Redesign workflows, not just training.** Training teaches skills; workflow redesign creates contexts where those skills get used. If AI remains a side activity—a separate tool that people have to remember to switch to—adoption will plateau. Embedding AI into existing tools and processes makes usage automatic.


## **The Stakes**

The conversion problem is severe. While only 40% of companies say they purchased an official LLM subscription, workers from over 90% of the companies surveyed reported regular use of personal AI tools for work tasks.

Workers are getting value. Organizations aren’t capturing it. The difference is the 201 layer.

The productivity gains are documented. The tools are available. The gap isn’t technology—it’s capability. Organizations that close the 201 gap will capture productivity gains at scale. Organizations that don’t will watch their best people get individual benefits while organizational capability stagnates.

The 201 layer isn’t about better prompts. It’s about building the judgment layer that makes AI-augmented work reliable and repeatable. It’s about moving from “some people use it sometimes” to “this is how we work now.” It’s about closing the gap between exploration and production.

That’s the 201 challenge. And it’s solvable—for organizations willing to invest in the middle layer that most training programs skip entirely.
